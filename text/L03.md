### 01.2 The simple linear regression model and its assumptions Slides p.3–4

This section writes the base model of the course. All inference results in this lecture are correct only when the four assumptions are true.

**What** Slides p.3–4

Simple Linear Regression · Lecture 3 · p.3–4 \[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i,\quad \epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\] Or: \[y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\] Assumptions:
1. Linearity
2. Independence
3. Normality
4. Equal variance (homoskedasticity)

The list below defines each symbol in the statement.

Simple linear regression (SLR) is a model in which the mean of a response \(y\) is a straight-line function of one explanatory variable \(x\).

The response \(y_i\) is the result value of observation \(i\). In the brainhead data, \(y_i\) is brain weight in g.

The explanatory variable \(x_i\) is the input value of observation \(i\). The model treats \(x_i\) as a known constant. In the brainhead data, \(x_i\) is head size in cm³.

The index \(i\) is the observation number, \(i=1,\dots,n\). The sample size \(n\) is the number of observations.

The intercept \(\beta_0\) and the slope \(\beta_1\) are two unknown constants of the population line. An unknown constant of a model is a parameter.

The error \(\epsilon_i\) is the difference between \(y_i\) and the population line \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\). It is random, and nobody can observe it.

\(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is the Normal distribution: a bell-shaped distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

The mean is the center of a distribution. The variance is the mean squared distance of the values from the mean.

\(\sigma^2\) is the error variance. It is the third unknown parameter. Its square root \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation.

"iid" means independent and identically distributed. Each \(\epsilon_i\) has the same distribution, and the value of one \(\epsilon_i\) does not change the distribution of another.

"indep" means independent. The second form does not say "identically distributed", because the mean \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) changes with \(x_i\).

The list below gives the meaning of each assumption.

1. Linearity: the mean of \(y\) is the straight-line function \(\beta_0+\beta_1x\).

2. Independence: the errors of different observations are independent.

3. Normality: each error has a Normal distribution.

4. Equal variance (homoskedasticity): the errors have the same variance \(\sigma^2\) at each value of \(x\).

The two forms give the same model. The first form writes "line plus error". The second form writes the distribution of \(y_i\).

**How** Added

Write a data set as an SLR model

- Find the response \(y\). It is the variable that the question explains or predicts.
- Find the explanatory variable \(x\). It is the variable that the question uses to explain \(y\).
- Write \(y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i\) with \(\epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\).
- List the three unknown parameters: \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\), \(\sigma^2\).
- For the second form, put the mean \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) and the variance \(\sigma^2\) into \(N(\cdot,\cdot)\).

**Self-check:** The only Greek letters in the model are \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\), \(\sigma^2\), and \(\epsilon_i\). The values \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) are data. The number \(n\) is equal to the number of data pairs.

**Example 1 · The small data set** Added

This example writes the model for the five pairs \((1,2),(2,4),(3,5),(4,4),(5,5)\).

Step 1–2: \(y\) is the second value of each pair. \(x\) is the first value. \(n=5\).

Step 3: \(y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i\), \(\epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\), \(i=1,\dots,5\).

Observation 3 gives the equation \(5=\beta_0+\beta_1\cdot3+\epsilon_3\). The values \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\), and \(\epsilon_3\) are unknown.

Step 5: \(y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\). For observation 3, \(y_3\sim N(\beta_0+3\beta_1,\sigma^2)\).

Thus, the model has three unknown parameters. Section 01.4 estimates \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), and Section 01.5 estimates \(\sigma^2\).

**Why · The two forms are the same** Added

The chain below finds the mean and the variance of \(y_i\) from the first form. Here \(x_i\), \(\beta_0\), and \(\beta_1\) are constants.

1

\(E[y_i]=E[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i]\)

2

\(=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+E[\epsilon_i]\)

linearity of expectation

3

\(=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+0\)

\(\epsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\)

4

\(\mathrm{Var}[y_i]=\mathrm{Var}[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i]\)

5

\(=\mathrm{Var}[\epsilon_i]\)

a constant does not change the variance

6

\(=\sigma^2\)

A Normal variable plus a constant is also Normal. Thus, \(y_i\sim N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\). The \(\epsilon_i\) are independent, so the \(y_i\) are also independent. ∎

### 01.3 The interpretation of \(\beta_1\) Slides p.5

This section gives the meaning of \(\beta_1\) in a real problem. The intervals and tests in Units 04–06 all give information about this quantity.

**What** Slides p.5

Interpretations · Lecture 3 · p.5 \[\beta_1=E[y|x=x^*+1]-E[y|x=x^*]\] \(\beta_1\) is the mean difference comparing a population with \(x\) to a population with \(x\) a unit lower
\(\hat\beta_1\) is an estimate of this mean difference

The conditional expectation \(E[y|x=x^*]\) is the mean of \(y\) in the group of units that has \(x=x^*\).

\(x^*\) is any fixed value of \(x\).

A population is the set of all possible units. Here "a population with \(x\)" is all units that have the same value of \(x\).

An estimate is a value that we calculate from the sample data. The hat symbol "^" shows that a quantity comes from the data. Thus, \(\hat\beta_1\) is the estimate of \(\beta_1\).

Compare two populations. The \(x\) of the first population is 1 unit higher than the \(x\) of the second. The difference between their mean values of \(y\) is \(\beta_1\).

**How** Added

Interpret \(\hat\beta_1\) in one sentence

- Find the unit of \(x\) and the unit of \(y\).
- Write: "Compare two populations whose \(x\) differs by 1 (unit of \(x\))."
- Continue: "The estimated mean of \(y\) differs by \(\hat\beta_1\) (unit of \(y\))."
- Use the sign of \(\hat\beta_1\) to say "higher" or "lower" for the population with the larger \(x\).
- For a difference of \(k\) units in \(x\), use the mean difference \(k\hat\beta_1\).

**Self-check:** The sentence contains the word "mean". It compares two populations. It gives the two units.

**Example 1 · The small data set** Added

Section 01.4 calculates \(\hat\beta_1=0.6\) for the small data set. This example uses that value. The data have no units.

Step 2–4: compare two populations whose \(x\) differs by 1. The estimated mean of \(y\) is 0.6 higher in the population with the larger \(x\).

Step 5: for \(x=5\) and \(x=1\), \(k=5-1=4\). The mean difference is \(4\times0.6=2.4\).

Thus, \(\hat\beta_1\) gives the change of the estimated mean for each unit of \(x\).

**Example 2 · The brainhead data** Added

This example uses \(\hat\beta_1=0.26082\) from Slides p.45.

Step 1: \(x\) is in cm³. \(y\) is in g.

Step 2–4: compare two populations whose head sizes differ by 1 cm³. The estimated mean brain weight is 0.26082 g higher in the population with the larger head size.

Step 5: for a difference of 100 cm³, the mean difference is \(100\times0.26082=26.082\) g.

This number is a difference of means. It does not give the brain weight change of one person.

Section 01.2 gives \(E[y|x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x\). The chain below puts in two values of \(x\) and subtracts.

1

\(E[y|x=x^*+1]-E[y|x=x^*]=\big(\beta_0+\beta_1(x^*+1)\big)-\big(\beta_0+\beta_1x^*\big)\)

put in \(E[y|x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x\)

2

\(=\beta_0+\beta_1x^*+\beta_1-\beta_0-\beta_1x^*\)

3

\(=\beta_1\)

### 01.4 Least squares estimation Slides p.6–7

This section calculates \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) from the data. The method selects the line with the smallest sum of squared vertical distances from the points.

**What** Slides p.6–7

Least Squares Estimation · Lecture 3 · p.6 Minimize the sum of squares: \[S(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2\] \[\Longrightarrow\ \frac{\partial S(\beta_0,\beta_1)}{\partial\beta_0}=0,\qquad \frac{\partial S(\beta_0,\beta_1)}{\partial\beta_1}=0\]

Estimates · Lecture 3 · p.7 So the least squares estimators are: \[\hat\beta_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x.\] OLS=MLE under the assumption of Normality
OLS well defined even without Normality, but only equal to MLE under Normality

The sum of squares \(S(\beta_0,\beta_1)\) is a function of a candidate line. For each point, it squares the vertical difference \(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i\). Then it adds the squares.

The partial derivative \(\partial S/\partial\beta_0\) is the derivative of \(S\) with respect to \(\beta_0\), with \(\beta_1\) held constant. At the minimum, both partial derivatives are 0.

Least squares (OLS, ordinary least squares) uses the values of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) that make \(S\) smallest.

An estimator is a formula that changes the data into an estimate. An example is \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\).

The sample means are \(\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\) and \(\bar y=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\).

\(S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2\) is the sum of squared deviations of \(x\). \(S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\) is the sum of cross products of deviations.

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) uses the parameter values that make the probability density of the observed data largest.

OLS needs only the sum of squares. Thus, OLS is defined without the Normality assumption. With the Normality assumption, OLS also gives the MLE.

**How** Added

Calculate \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_0\)

- Calculate \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\).
- Calculate the deviations \(x_i-\bar x\) and \(y_i-\bar y\) for each observation.
- Calculate \(S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\).
- Calculate \(S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\).
- Calculate \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\).
- Calculate \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\).

**Self-check:** \(S_{xx}>0\). The sign of \(\hat\beta_1\) is the sign of \(S_{xy}\). The point \((\bar x,\bar y)\) is on the fitted line: \(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x=\bar y\).

This example applies the six steps to \(x=(1,2,3,4,5)\) and \(y=(2,4,5,4,5)\).

Step 3–4: the two sums.

1

\(S_{xx}=(-2)^2+(-1)^2+0^2+1^2+2^2\)

2

\(=4+1+0+1+4=10\)

3

Step 5–6: the slope and the intercept.

### 01.5 Fitted values, residuals, and \(\hat\sigma^2\) Slides p.8

This section estimates the third parameter, \(\sigma^2\). First, it calculates the residual of each point. Then it adds the squared residuals and divides by \(n-2\). Units 04–06 use \(\hat\sigma\) in the standard error and in the \(t\) statistic.

**What** Slides p.8

Fitted Values, Residuals, and σ̂² · Lecture 3 · p.8 Fitted values: \[\hat y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i\] Residuals: \[e_i=y_i-\hat y_i=y_i-(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i)\] • Note: \(e_i\) are different from errors, \(\epsilon_i=y_i-(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\), and have different properties
Estimate of \(\sigma^2\) is: \[\hat\sigma^2=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{n-2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2}{n-2}\] • This is not the MLE—instead it is an unbiased estimate of \(\sigma^2\)

The fitted value \(\hat y_i\) is the height of the fitted line at \(x=x_i\).

The residual \(e_i\) is the observed value minus the fitted value: \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). It uses the fitted line, so we can calculate it.

The error \(\epsilon_i\) is the observed value minus the population line. The population line is unknown, so we cannot calculate \(\epsilon_i\). The residual \(e_i\) is the calculable replacement for \(\epsilon_i\).

The sum of squared residuals \(\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2\) is the minimum value of \(S\). It occurs at the least squares line.

An estimator is unbiased when its expected value is equal to the parameter. Here, \(E[\hat\sigma^2]=\sigma^2\).

\(\hat\sigma\) is the square root of \(\hat\sigma^2\). It has the same unit as \(y\).

**How** Added

Calculate \(\hat y_i\), \(e_i\), and \(\hat\sigma^2\)

- Calculate each fitted value \(\hat y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i\).
- Calculate each residual \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\).
- Square each residual. Add the squares to get \(\sum e_i^2\).
- Divide the sum by \(n-2\). The result is \(\hat\sigma^2\).
- Take the square root of \(\hat\sigma^2\). The result is \(\hat\sigma\).

**Self-check:** \(\sum e_i=0\), with a small rounding difference. \(\hat\sigma^2>0\). The denominator is \(n-2\), not \(n\).

### 02.4 Write \(\hat\beta_1\) as a linear combination of the \(y_i\) Slides p.12

This section writes \(\hat\beta_1\) as \(\sum w_iy_i\). With this form, we can get the distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) from the distribution of the \(y_i\).

**What** Slides p.12

Properties of \(\hat\beta_1\) · Lecture 3 · p.12 (slide 8/25) Recall: \(y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\) \[\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\sum_i y_i(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}=\sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i\] where \(w_i=\frac{(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}\) are fixed with respect to \(y\).
Hence \(\hat\beta_1\) is a linear combination of independent Normals!

The slide uses these symbols and names.

\(y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\): each \(y_i\) has a Normal distribution with mean \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). The \(y_i\) are independent of each other.

Independent: the value of one \(y_i\) gives no information about the value of a different \(y_i\).

\(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\): the sample means of \(x\) and \(y\).

The denominator \(\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\) is \(S_{xx}\). Its index is \(j\), so that it is different from the index \(i\) in the numerator.

Weight \(w_i\): the number that multiplies \(y_i\) in \(\hat\beta_1\).

Fixed with respect to \(y\): \(w_i\) uses only \(x_1,\dots,x_n\). The model treats each \(x_i\) as a known constant, so \(w_i\) is a constant.

Linear combination: a sum of quantities, each multiplied by a constant, for example \(a_1y_1+a_2y_2+\dots+a_ny_n\). Here the constants are the \(w_i\).

Thus p.12 says: \(\hat\beta_1\) is a sum of independent Normal variables \(y_i\), each multiplied by a constant \(w_i\).

**How** Added

Calculate the \(w_i\) and use them to get \(\hat\beta_1\)

- Calculate \(\bar x\).
- Calculate \(S_{xx}=\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\).
- For each \(i\), calculate \(w_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}\).
- Calculate \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i\).
- Compare the result of step 4 with \(S_{xy}/S_{xx}\).

**Self-check:** (1) \(\sum w_iy_i\) must equal \(S_{xy}/S_{xx}\). (2) \(\sum w_i\) must equal 0. (3) \(\sum w_ix_i\) must equal 1.

### 02.5 The sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) Slides p.13

This section gives the full distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\). It uses the result of Section 02.4: \(\hat\beta_1\) is a linear combination of independent Normal variables.

**What** Slides p.13

Properties of \(\hat\beta_1\) · Lecture 3 · p.13 (slide 8/25) Hence \(\hat\beta_1\) is a linear combination of independent Normals! \[\Longrightarrow\ \hat\beta_1\sim N\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i),\ \sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2\Big)\]

Sampling distribution: the distribution of the values of an estimator over all possible samples. Here the estimator is \(\hat\beta_1\).

Expectation \(E[\cdot]\): the mean value of a random variable over many repeated samples. The expectation of \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is \(\mu\).

The variance \(\mathrm{Var}[\cdot]\) measures spread (Section 01.2). The variance of \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is \(\sigma^2\).

Thus p.13 says: \(\hat\beta_1\) is Normal, with expectation \(\sum w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\) and variance \(\sigma^2\sum w_i^2\).

This step uses one rule. A linear combination of independent Normal variables is also Normal. If \(y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)\) and each \(a_i\) is a constant, then \(\sum a_iy_i\sim N(\sum a_i\mu_i,\ \sum a_i^2\sigma_i^2)\).

**How** Added

Write the distribution of a linear combination

- Write the estimator as \(\sum a_iy_i\). Make sure that no \(a_i\) contains \(y\). Here \(a_i=w_i\).
- Make sure that the \(y_i\) are independent and Normal. Here the model gives this.
- Write the mean \(\mu_i\) and the variance \(\sigma_i^2\) of each \(y_i\). Here \(\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) and \(\sigma_i^2=\sigma^2\).
- Calculate the mean \(\sum a_i\mu_i\).
- Calculate the variance \(\sum a_i^2\sigma_i^2\).
- Write the result as \(N(\text{mean},\ \text{variance})\).

**Self-check:** The variance uses squared constants (\(w_i^2\)). The mean uses the constants without squares (\(w_i\)). The variance must be positive.

### 02.6 The mean of \(\hat\beta_1\): unbiasedness Slides p.14–18

This section simplifies the mean \(\sum w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\) from Section 02.5. The result is \(\beta_1\). Thus, on average, \(\hat\beta_1\) is equal to the true value.

**What** Slides p.14–18

Mean of \(\hat\beta_1\) · Lecture 3 · p.14–18 (slide 9/25) \[\begin{aligned}E[\hat\beta_1]&=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\\&=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\\&=\beta_0\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}+\beta_1\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}\\&=0+\beta_1\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}\\&=\beta_1\end{aligned}\] Hence it is unbiased.

One single \(\hat\beta_1\) can still be different from \(\beta_1\).

### 03.1 Variance of \(\hat\beta_1\) Slides p.19–23

This section tells how much \(\hat\beta_1\) changes from sample to sample. The answer is a formula that uses only \(\sigma^2\) and the \(x\) values.

\(S_{xx}=\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\): the sum of the squared distances between each \(x_j\) and \(\bar x\). It uses only the \(x\) values.

**What** Slides p.19–23

Variance of \(\hat\beta_1\) · Lecture 3 · p.19–23 \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]&=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2\\ &=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\frac{(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}\right]^2\\ &=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(x_i-\bar x)^2}{\left[\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\right]^2}\\ &=\sigma^2\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}{\left[\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\right]^2}\\ &=\sigma^2\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} \end{aligned}\]

On Slides p.23, the denominator of the last line is \(\sum_{j=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2\). The sum index is \(j\). Thus the term must be \(x_j\). The block above uses \(x_j\).

The variance of \(\hat\beta_1\) is equal to the error variance \(\sigma^2\) divided by \(S_{xx}\).

When \(\sigma^2\) increases, the points are farther from the line. Then the variance of \(\hat\beta_1\) increases.

When \(S_{xx}\) increases, the \(x\) values have a larger spread. Then the variance of \(\hat\beta_1\) decreases.

\(S_{xx}\) is a sum of \(n\) terms, and each term is 0 or more. An added observation cannot make \(S_{xx}\) smaller.

### 03.2 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) Slides p.24

### 03.3 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\) Slides p.24

Slides p.24 then asks: "what about \(\hat\beta_0\)?" This section gives the answer and calculates the values for the 6 observations.

**What** Slides p.24

Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\) · Lecture 3 · p.24 Question: what about \(\hat\beta_0\)? \[\hat\beta_0\sim N\Big(\beta_0,\ \sigma^2\Big(\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\Big)\Big)\]

\(\hat\beta_0\) is also Normal and unbiased. Its mean is the true value \(\beta_0\). Its variance is \(\sigma^2\) multiplied by the sum of two terms.

The first term is \(\frac1n\). When the sample size increases, this term decreases.

The second term is \(\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\). When \(\bar x\) is farther from 0, this term increases.

The intercept \(\beta_0\) is the mean of \(y\) at \(x=0\). When the data are far from \(x=0\), the estimate of \(\beta_0\) is less precise.

**How** Added

Covariance: \(\mathrm{Cov}[U,V]=E[(U-E[U])(V-E[V])]\). It shows how two random variables change together.

For two random variables, \(\mathrm{Var}[U-V]=\mathrm{Var}[U]+\mathrm{Var}[V]-2\mathrm{Cov}[U,V]\).

\(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\bar x\,\hat\beta_1\). Thus \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_0]=\mathrm{Var}[\bar y]+\bar x^2\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]-2\bar x\,\mathrm{Cov}[\bar y,\hat\beta_1]\).

\(\mathrm{Var}[\bar y]=\sigma^2/n\), and \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\).

\(\mathrm{Cov}[\bar y,\hat\beta_1]=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1n w_i\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i=0\), because \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=0\).

Put these together: \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_0]=\sigma^2\big(\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\big)\). ∎

### 04.1 Standardize \(\hat\beta_1\) Slides p.25–26

This section changes \(\hat\beta_1\) into a quantity \(Z\) with the fixed distribution \(N(0,1)\). All the confidence intervals in this unit start from this \(Z\).

**What** Slides p.26

Confidence interval for β1 · Lecture 3 · p.26 \[\hat\beta_1\sim N\!\left(\beta_1,\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim N(0,1)\]

Confidence interval (CI): an interval that we calculate from the data. The method makes the interval contain the unknown parameter with a given probability, for example 95%.

Standardize: subtract the mean of a random variable, then divide by its standard deviation. The result has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

The standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\) is \(\sqrt{\sigma^2/S_{xx}}=\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\). Thus the slide subtracts the mean \(\beta_1\) and divides by \(\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).

The distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) contains the unknown values \(\beta_1\) and \(\sigma\). After we standardize, the distribution is \(N(0,1)\). This distribution contains no unknown values. Thus we can use known probabilities of \(N(0,1)\) to make statements about \(\beta_1\).

This section gives \(Z=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim N(0,1)\). The How steps below calculate each part of \(Z\) from data.

**How** Added

Write \(Z\) for a data set

The derivation uses one fact: a linear transformation of a Normal variable is also Normal. A linear transformation multiplies by a constant and adds a constant. Then the derivation calculates the mean and the variance of \(Z\).

\[Z=\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}=\frac{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}{\sigma}\hat\beta_1-\frac{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}{\sigma}\beta_1\]

**Substitute** \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\).

\[=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z\sim N(0,1)\]

### 04.2 95% CI for \(\beta_1\) with known \(\sigma\) Slides p.27–30

This section uses a known \(\sigma\). It starts from \(P(-1.96\le Z\le1.96)=0.95\) and changes the inequality until \(\beta_1\) is alone in the middle. The result is the 95% confidence interval.

**What** Slides p.27–30

95% CI for β1 — Known σ · Lecture 3 · p.27–30 Suppose \(\sigma\) is known. \[0.95=P(-1.96\le Z\le1.96)\] \[0.95=P\!\left(\hat\beta_1-1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\le\beta_1\le\hat\beta_1+1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\right)\] So a 95% CI for \(\beta_1\) is \(\hat\beta_1\pm1.96\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\)
I.e. the random interval \(\hat\beta_1\pm1.96\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) will cover the true \(\beta_1\) 95% of the time.

\(P(A)\): the probability of the event \(A\). It is a number from 0 to 1.

Quantile: for a random variable \(W\) and a probability \(p\), the quantile is the number \(q\) with \(P(W\le q)=p\).

1.96 is the 0.975 quantile of \(N(0,1)\): \(P(Z\le1.96)=0.975\). The more exact value is 1.959964.

\(\pm\): "plus or minus". \(a\pm b\) is the interval from \(a-b\) to \(a+b\).

Random interval: its end points come from the random value \(\hat\beta_1\), so the interval changes with the data. \(\beta_1\) is a fixed number.

\(N(0,1)\) is symmetric about 0. Each tail outside \(\pm1.96\) has area 0.025, so the center has area 0.95. We put the definition of \(Z\) into the inequality and solve for \(\beta_1\). "95%" is a property of the method. With many new samples and one interval per sample, about 95% of the intervals contain \(\beta_1\).

This section gives the formula \(\hat\beta_1\pm1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\). The How steps below use it.

### 04.3 Unknown \(\sigma\): use \(\hat\sigma\) and get the t distribution Slides p.31–33

This section replaces \(\sigma\) in \(Z\) with \(\hat\sigma\) from the data. The new quantity does not have the distribution \(N(0,1)\). It has a t distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. This distribution is fully known, so we can still make an interval.

**What** Slides p.31–33

95% CI for β1 — Unknown σ · Lecture 3 · p.31–33 In practice, \(\sigma\) is unknown and must be estimated. We have:
1. \(Z=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim N(0,1)\)
2. \(V=\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2\sim\chi^2_{(n-2)}\)
3. It can be shown that \(Z\) and \(V\) are independent
Note that for independent \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\), \(\Longrightarrow\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}\sim t_\nu\) \[\Longrightarrow\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}=\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\] \[\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{1/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\] \[\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\]

The chi-squared distribution \(\chi^2_\nu\) (Section 01.5) is the distribution of a sum of \(\nu\) squared independent \(N(0,1)\) variables.

Degrees of freedom (df) \(\nu\): the parameter of a \(\chi^2\) or t distribution. It sets the shape. Here \(\nu=n-2\), the same as the denominator of \(\hat\sigma^2\).

t distribution \(t_\nu\): the distribution of \(Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}\), with independent \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\).

The t distribution is bell-shaped and symmetric about 0. Its tails are thicker than the tails of \(N(0,1)\). When \(\nu\) increases, it comes nearer to \(N(0,1)\).

The slides state item 3 (independence of \(Z\) and \(V\)) without proof.

We cannot calculate \(Z\) from data, because \(Z\) contains the unknown \(\sigma\). We replace \(\sigma\) with \(\hat\sigma\). Then the denominator is also random, and the distribution changes from \(N(0,1)\) to \(t_{(n-2)}\). The final quantity \(\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) does not contain \(\sigma\). Its distribution \(t_{(n-2)}\) contains no unknown values.

This section gives the quantity \(\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\), which we can use with data. The How steps below calculate it.

\[\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-2)}}=\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\]

**Substitute** the definitions of \(Z\) and \(V\).

3

\[=\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\]

\(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^2}A}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{A}\) because \(\sigma>0\).

4

\[=\frac{\sigma\cdot\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\]

5

\[=\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{1/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}}\]

\(\sigma\) cancels here. This is the second line of Slides p.32.

6

\[=\frac{\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{1/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}{\hat\sigma}\]

**Substitute** \(\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2/(n-2)}\) (Slides p.8).

7

\[=\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\]

This is the last line of Slides p.33. ∎

\(\sigma\) cancels, so we can use \(\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\) with data. Section 04.4 uses its quantile to make the interval.

### 04.4 Make the interval with a t quantile \(q=t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\) Slides p.34

This section does the same inequality steps as Section 04.2. The difference: 1.96 changes to a quantile \(q\) of \(t_{(n-2)}\), and \(\sigma\) changes to \(\hat\sigma\).

**What** Slides p.34

95% CI for β1 — Unknown σ · Lecture 3 · p.34 So we can find some quantile \(q\) such that \[0.95=P\!\left(-q\le\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\le q\right)\] \[0.95=P\!\left(\hat\beta_1-q\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\le\beta_1\le\hat\beta_1+q\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\right)\] and construct a 95% CI for \(\beta_1\) is \(\hat\beta_1\pm q\dfrac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\)
Often written \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\), as in: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\dfrac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\)

\(\alpha\) (alpha): the probability that the interval does not contain the true value. The confidence level is \(1-\alpha\). A 95% CI has \(\alpha=0.05\).

\(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\): the \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile of \(t_{(n-2)}\). Thus \(P(T\le t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2})=1-\alpha/2\), and the right tail has area \(\alpha/2\). Here \(T\) is a \(t_{(n-2)}\) variable.

For a 95% CI, \(\alpha/2=0.025\). Thus \(q\) is the 0.975 quantile \(t_{0.975,n-2}\). We get it from a t table or from statistical software.

The t distribution is also symmetric about 0. Each tail outside \(\pm q\) has area \(\alpha/2\), so the center has area \(1-\alpha\). The t tails are thicker than the Normal tails. Thus \(q\) is a little larger than 1.96, and the interval is a little wider.

This section gives the interval for practice: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\). The How steps below find \(q\).

**How** Added

Find \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\) and write the interval

- **Get \(\alpha\).** \(\alpha=1-\) confidence level. For 95%, \(\alpha=0.05\).
- **Get the degrees of freedom.** df \(=n-2\).
- **Get \(q\).** Find the \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile of \(t_{(n-2)}\) in a t table or with software.
- **Write the interval.** \(\hat\beta_1\pm q\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).

Self-check: ① For 95%, \(q\) is larger than 1.96. When df increases, \(q\) comes nearer to 1.96. ② The left tail area \(P(T\le-q)\) must equal \(\alpha/2\).

### 04.5 Standard deviation and standard error: \(\mathrm{SD}(\hat\beta_1)\) and \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) Slides p.35

This section names two quantities. The first is the true standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\), which contains \(\sigma\). The second is its estimate, the standard error, which contains \(\hat\sigma\). The interval uses the standard error.

**What** Slides p.35

Standard Errors · Lecture 3 · p.35 Standard error of \(\hat\beta_1\) is the estimated standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\) \[\mathrm{SD}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\] \[\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\] Can write a CI as \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\)

\(\mathrm{SD}(\hat\beta_1)\): the standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\). It shows the usual distance of \(\hat\beta_1\) from \(\beta_1\) over many samples. It contains the unknown \(\sigma\), so we cannot calculate it.

Standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\): the estimate of \(\mathrm{SD}(\hat\beta_1)\) with \(\hat\sigma\) in place of \(\sigma\). We can calculate it from data.

In the interval of Section 04.4, \(\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) is \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). Thus the interval has the form "estimate ± t quantile × standard error".

This section gives the form on the slide: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). The How steps below calculate SE.

**How** Added

Calculate \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\)

- **Calculate \(\hat\sigma\).** \(\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\sum e_i^2/(n-2)}\) (Section 04.3, How steps 2–4).
- **Calculate \(\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).** Use Section 04.1, How step 3.
- **Divide.** \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).

Self-check: SE has the same unit as \(\hat\beta_1\). SE uses \(\hat\sigma\), not \(\sigma\).

### 05.3 Interpretation of the confidence interval Slides p.39

This section shows what "95%" describes. The answer: it describes the **method** that makes the interval. It does not describe one interval that we already calculated.

**What** Slides p.39

Interval interpretation · Lecture 3 · p.39 If we were to draw samples repeatedly and construct intervals in the same way, any given interval may or may not cover the truth. On average, 95% will cover the truth.

\(\beta_1\) is a fixed number. It does not change from sample to sample. It has no probability distribution.

Before we take the sample, the limits \(L=\hat\beta_1-t_{0.975,\,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) and \(U=\hat\beta_1+t_{0.975,\,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) come from the random \(y_i\). Thus \(L\) and \(U\) are random variables.

An interval covers \(\beta_1\) when \(L\le\beta_1\le U\).

"95%" means \(P(L\le\beta_1\le U)=0.95\). Over many samples, 95% of the intervals cover the true \(\beta_1\).

After we take the sample, \(L=0.235\) and \(U=0.287\) are fixed numbers. This one interval covers \(\beta_1\), or it does not cover \(\beta_1\).

We say that we have 95% confidence in [0.235, 0.287]. This means that the interval comes from a method that covers \(\beta_1\) in 95% of samples.

**How** Added

Write the interpretation of a confidence interval

- State the parameter and its unit.
- State the interval and the confidence level.
- Say that the 95% describes the method. Use repeated samples and the same method.

### 06.1 Hypothesis test setup and test statistic Slides p.40–41

This section writes the two claims to test. Then it finds a quantity with a fully known distribution when the null hypothesis is true.

Slides p.40 is the section page "Hypothesis Tests for \(\beta_1\)". It has no formulas.

Hypothesis test: a method that uses data to measure the evidence against a claim about a parameter.

Null hypothesis \(H_0\): the claim that we test, for example \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\). The test starts with the assumption that \(H_0\) is true.

\(\theta_0\): a specified number from the question. The most frequent value is \(\theta_0=0\). Then \(H_0\) says that the mean of \(y\) has no linear relation with \(x\).

Alternative hypothesis \(H_1\): the claim against \(H_0\), for example \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\).

Test statistic: a number that we calculate from the data. Its distribution under \(H_0\) is known. Thus we can tell if its value is "extreme".

**What** Slides p.40–41

Hypothesis Testing · Lecture 3 · p.41 Want to test a null hypothesis \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\) vs. some alternative hypothesis \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\).
• E.g., often want to test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\).
Goal is to characterize how much evidence we have against \(H_0\)
• How “extreme” are our data relative to \(H_0\)?
• I.e. If \(H_0\) really were true, would these data be really surprising?
Under \(H_0\): \[\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\]

The test does not prove that \(H_0\) is true or false. The test measures how much evidence the data give against \(H_0\). We assume that \(H_0\) is true. Then we ask if the observed data are rare under that assumption.

The test statistic \(\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) gives the distance from \(\hat\beta_1\) to \(\theta_0\) in units of standard errors. A larger distance shows that the data agree less with \(H_0\).

Notation: \(T\) is the random test statistic. \(t\) is its observed value from our data (Slides p.42).

**How** Added

Calculate the observed test statistic \(t\)

- Write \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\). Get \(\theta_0\) from the question.
- Calculate \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\).
- Calculate \(\hat\sigma\). Then calculate \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).
- Calculate \(t=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\).
- Write the reference distribution \(t_{(n-2)}\). The degrees of freedom are \(n-2\).

**Self-check:** When \(\hat\beta_1>\theta_0\), \(t\) must be positive. When \(\hat\beta_1<\theta_0\), \(t\) must be negative. The denominator \(\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) must be positive.

### 06.2 The p-value Slides p.42

This section changes the observed test statistic into a probability, the p-value. A smaller p-value shows less support for \(H_0\).

p-value: the probability, under \(H_0\), that the test statistic is as extreme as the observed value or more extreme.

Extreme: in a two-sided test, a large \(|T|\). Thus \(T\) is far from 0 in either direction.

Probability density function (PDF): a curve. The area under the curve over an interval is the probability that the random variable is in that interval.

### 06.5 Interpret the p-value Slides p.46–47

This section gives the correct interpretation of a p-value. Then it defines the Type-I error rate.

Type-I error: \(H_0\) is true, but the test rejects \(H_0\).

Type-I error rate: the probability that the test rejects \(H_0\) when \(H_0\) is true.

**What** Slides p.46–47

Interpreting P-Values · Lecture 3 · p.46–47 Does this mean pval \(=P(\beta_1=0)\)? No! Instead, it means that under the null hypothesis (i.e. assuming \(\beta_1=0\)) the probability of a test statistic as extreme as the one observed is pval. That’s why a small p-value is evidence against the null, since it would be particularly “rare” under the null (and hence surprising).

If we reject the null whenever we get a p-value below 5%, then if the null is true and we repeat the experiment over and over then we will only reject the null incorrectly 5% of the time. This is called the Type-I error rate.

We calculate the p-value with the assumption \(\beta_1=0\). The p-value tells how rare the observed test statistic is. \(\beta_1\) is a fixed constant. Thus the p-value is a probability about the data, not about \(\beta_1\).

Use the rule "reject when the p-value is below 5%". Assume that \(H_0\) is true and that we repeat the experiment many times. Then 5% of the tests reject \(H_0\) incorrectly.

**How** Added

Interpret a p-value in one sentence

- Write the condition: "Assuming \(H_0\) is true (\(\beta_1=\theta_0\)), …".
- Write the event: "… the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed \(t\) …".
- Write the value: "… is p-value = …".
- Write the result: a small p-value shows rare data under \(H_0\). Thus it is evidence against \(H_0\).

**Self-check:** The sentence must contain the condition "assuming \(H_0\)". The probability must be about the test statistic.

**Why · The Type-I error rate is 5%** Slides p.47

This part shows that the rule "reject when the p-value is below 0.05" rejects with probability 0.05 when \(H_0\) is true. It uses the quantile \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\).

Section 04.4 defines the quantile \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\). For the brainhead data, \(t_{0.975,\,234}=1.970154\).

1

\[P(\text{reject}\mid H_0)=P(\text{p-value}<0.05\mid H_0)\]

The decision rule.

2

\[\text{p-value}<0.05\iff 2P(T\ge|t|)<0.05\]

Section 06.2: \(P(|T|\ge|t|)=2P(T\ge|t|)\).

3

\[2P(T\ge|t|)<0.05\iff P(T\ge|t|)<0.025\]

4

\[P(T\ge|t|)<0.025\iff |t|>t_{0.975,\,n-2}\]

The right-tail area decreases when \(|t|\) increases. The area 0.025 is at the point \(t_{0.975,\,n-2}\).

5

\[P(\text{reject}\mid H_0)=P(|T|>t_{0.975,\,n-2})\]

Under \(H_0\), \(T\sim t_{(n-2)}\).

6

\[P(|T|>t_{0.975,\,n-2})=0.025+0.025=0.05\]

Each tail has area 0.025 (symmetry).

∎ Thus the limit 5% gives a Type-I error rate of 5%. All tests so far are two-sided. The next section gives one-sided tests.

### 06.6 One-sided tests Slides p.48–49

This section uses an alternative hypothesis in one direction only. The test statistic does not change. The p-value uses only one tail.

Two-sided alternative: \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\). Evidence for \(\beta_1>\theta_0\) and evidence for \(\beta_1<\theta_0\) both count against \(H_0\).

One-sided alternative: for example \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\). Only evidence that \(\beta_1\) is larger than \(\theta_0\) counts.

**What** Slides p.48–49

One-sided hypothesis tests · Lecture 3 · p.48–49 Above we conducted a hypothesis test of the null \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\) vs the two-sided alternative \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\)
• Evidence against the null (and in favour of the alternative) is evidence that either \(\beta_1>\theta_0\) or \(\beta_1<\theta_0\)
Sometimes we are interested in evidence one direction: e.g., \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\)
• Evidence against the null in favour of \(H_1\) is evidence that the true \(\beta_1\) is larger than \(\theta_0\)
• How do we compute a p-value here?
◦ Consider the test statistic: \(\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\)
◦ Evidence against the null and in favour of \(H_1\) is evidence that \(\beta_1>\theta_0\).
◦ So large values of \(\hat\beta_1-\theta_0\) indicate evidence against the null in favour of \(H_1\)
◦ \(\Longrightarrow\) large values of \(\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) indicate evidence in favour of \(H_1\)
◦ I.e. p-value only includes one tail

Note: Slides p.48 writes the two-sided alternative as \(H_1:\theta\ne\theta_0\). The meaning is \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\). The block above uses this form.

For \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\), only large positive values of \(t\) support \(H_1\). Thus "as extreme or more extreme" means only the right side. The p-value is \(P(T\ge t)\). Do not take the absolute value. Do not multiply by 2.

- Find the right-tail area \(P(T\ge t)\) for \(T\sim t_{(n-2)}\).

**Why · The p-value uses one tail** Slides p.49

This part shows why the one-sided p-value is \(P(T\ge t)\). It follows the arrow chain on Slides p.49.

1

\[H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\]

Evidence for \(H_1\) is evidence that \(\beta_1\) is larger than \(\theta_0\).

2

\[\hat\beta_1-\theta_0\ \text{large}\]

\(\hat\beta_1\) is unbiased for \(\beta_1\). Thus \(\beta_1>\theta_0\) usually gives large positive \(\hat\beta_1-\theta_0\).

3

\[\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\ \text{large}\]

The denominator is positive. It does not change the direction.

4

\[\text{p-value}=P(T\ge t)\quad\text{under }H_0,\ T\sim t_{(n-2)}\]

"As extreme or more extreme" includes only values larger than \(t\): the right tail.

∎ When \(t>0\), the symmetry in Section 06.2 gives \(P(T\ge t)=\tfrac12P(|T|\ge|t|)\). This section gives the one-sided p-value: the area of the right tail.

**Q1.** For the brainhead data, \(\hat\beta_1=0.2608207\), \(\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}=0.01306914\), and \(n=236\). Test \(H_0:\beta_1=0.25\) vs. \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0.25\) at the 5% level. Give the test statistic, the p-value, and the result.

Answer

\(t=\dfrac{0.2608207-0.25}{0.01306914}=\dfrac{0.0108207}{0.01306914}=0.8280\).

The reference distribution is \(t_{(234)}\). The right tail is \(P(T\ge0.8280)=0.2043\). The p-value is \(2\times0.2043=0.4085\).

\(0.4085>0.05\). We do not have enough evidence to reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0.25\) at the 5% level.

**Q2.** Use the same data. A researcher says that the slope is larger than 0.24. Test \(H_0:\beta_1=0.24\) vs. \(H_1:\beta_1>0.24\) at the 5% level. Also give the two-sided p-value for \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0.24\).

Answer

\(t=\dfrac{0.2608207-0.24}{0.01306914}=\dfrac{0.0208207}{0.01306914}=1.5931\).

The one-sided p-value is \(P(T\ge1.5931)=0.05624\), with \(T\sim t_{(234)}\).

\(0.05624>0.05\). We do not have enough evidence to reject \(H_0\) in favour of \(\beta_1>0.24\) at the 5% level.

The two-sided p-value is \(2\times0.05624=0.1125\). The one-sided p-value is half of it.
