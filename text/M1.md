### 01.2 Lecture 1: summaries and four distributions Slides L1 p.10–20, p.25–28

This section lists the summaries of one or two variables and the four distributions that later lectures use.

Random variable: a quantity whose value comes from chance, for example \(Y\). Expectation (mean) \(E[Y]\): the long-run average value of \(Y\). Variance \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)\): the mean squared distance of \(Y\) from its mean.

| Formula | Meaning | Source

| \(E[Y]=\int y f(y)\,dy\) | Mean of \(Y\). Here \(f\) is the PDF (probability density function) of \(Y\). | L1 p.10 · L01 unit 02

| \(E\left[\sum_{i=1}^m(a_iY_i+b_i)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^m a_iE[Y_i]+\sum_{i=1}^m b_i\) | Linearity of expectation. It is true for all random variables, also for dependent ones. | L1 p.11 · L01 unit 02

| \(\bar y=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\) | Sample mean of the observations \(y_1,\dots,y_n\). | L1 p.12 · L01 unit 02

| \(\mathrm{Var}[Y]=E[(Y-E[Y])^2]=E[Y^2]-E[Y]^2\) | Variance. The second form is faster to calculate. | L1 p.13 · L01 unit 02

| \(\mathrm{Var}(aY+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(Y)\) | A shift \(b\) does not change the spread. A scale \(a\) multiplies the variance by \(a^2\). | L1 p.14 · L01 unit 02

| \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)\) | Only for **independent** \(X\) and \(Y\). | L1 p.14 · L01 unit 02

| \(s_y^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2\) | Sample variance. The divisor is \(n-1\). | L1 p.15 · L01 unit 02

| \(\mathrm{cov}[X,Y]=E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\) | Covariance: a measure of how \(X\) and \(Y\) change together. Positive means that they increase together. | L1 p.17 · L01 unit 03

| \(\mathrm{cov}(X,X)=\mathrm{Var}(X)\)
\(\mathrm{cov}(aY+c,bX+d)=ab\,\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\)
\(\mathrm{cov}(U+V,X+Y)=\mathrm{cov}(U,X)+\mathrm{cov}(U,Y)+\mathrm{cov}(V,X)+\mathrm{cov}(V,Y)\) | Rules of covariance. Constants \(c,d\) drop out. Sums expand term by term. | L1 p.18 · L01 unit 03

| \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\,\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\) | Answer to the exercise on L1 p.18. It is true for all \(X,Y\). Added | L01 unit 03

| \(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)(x_i-\bar x)\) | Sample covariance. | L1 p.19 · L01 unit 03

| \(\rho=\dfrac{\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(X)}\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(Y)}}\) | Correlation: covariance without units. It measures the strength of a **linear** relation. Its value is from \(-1\) to \(1\). | L1 p.20 · L01 unit 03

| \(r=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}\) | Sample correlation. The factors \(\frac{1}{n-1}\) cancel. | L1 p.20 · L01 unit 03

| \(S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2-n\bar x^2\)
\(S_{yy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2\)
\(S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)(x_i-\bar x)=\sum_{i=1}^n y_ix_i-n\bar y\bar x\) | Sums of squares and cross-products. The second forms come from L2 p.25–26. Use them when the exam gives \(\sum x_i^2\) or \(\sum x_iy_i\). | L1 p.20, L2 p.25–26 · L02 unit 03

| \(S_{xx}=(n-1)s_x^2\) | Links \(S_{xx}\) to the sample variance of \(x\) (L4 Practice Q1(a)). Added | L04 unit 06

**Distributions** Slides L1 p.25–28

Degrees of freedom (df): the parameter \(\nu\) of a chi-square, t or F distribution. For a t distribution, a large \(\nu\) makes it close to \(N(0,1)\).

| Distribution | Facts on the slides | Source

| Normal \(Z\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) | \(f(z)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left[-\dfrac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\), \(E[Z]=\mu\), \(\mathrm{Var}(Z)=\sigma^2\).
For independent \(Z_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)\): \(U=\sum_i(a_iZ_i+b_i)\sim N\left(\sum_i(a_i\mu_i+b_i),\ \sum_i a_i^2\sigma_i^2\right)\). | L1 p.25 · L01 unit 05

| Chi-square \(X\sim\chi^2_\nu\) | \(E[X]=\nu\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=2\nu\), \(X>0\).
For \(Z_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,1)\): \(\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2\sim\chi^2_n\). | L1 p.26 · L01 unit 05

| t \(Y\sim t_\nu\) | \(E[Y]=0\) if \(\nu>1\). \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\dfrac{\nu}{\nu-2}\) if \(\nu>2\).
For **independent** \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(X\sim\chi^2_\nu\): \(\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{X/\nu}}\sim t_\nu\). | L1 p.27 · L01 unit 05

| F \(W\sim F(\nu_1,\nu_2)\) | \(w>0\). For **independent** \(X_1\sim\chi^2_{\nu_1}\) and \(X_2\sim\chi^2_{\nu_2}\): \(W=\dfrac{X_1/\nu_1}{X_2/\nu_2}\sim F(\nu_1,\nu_2)\). | L1 p.28 · L01 unit 05

| Standardize | If \(Z\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(\dfrac{Z-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\). This is the normal rule with \(a=1/\sigma\), \(b=-\mu/\sigma\). Added | L03 unit 04

### 01.3 Lecture 2: the model, least squares, MLE, residuals, \(\sigma^2\) Slides L2 p.13–36

This section lists the simple linear regression (SLR) model and the estimates of its three parameters.

Simple Linear Regression · Lecture 2 · p.13, p.18 \[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i,\quad \epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\qquad\text{Or:}\qquad y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\]
\(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2\): fixed, unknown parameters. \(\epsilon_i\) is unobserved random error term. \(y_i,x_i\) are the observed data. Treat \(x_i\) as fixed.

Assumptions: 1. Linearity 2. Independence 3. Normality 4. Equal variance (homoskedasticity)

| Assumption | Meaning in one line

| 1. Linearity | The mean of \(y\) is a straight line in \(x\): \(E[y_i|x_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), so \(E[\epsilon_i]=0\).

| 2. Independence | The errors \(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_n\) are independent.

| 3. Normality | Each error has a normal distribution.

| 4. Equal variance | All errors have the same variance \(\sigma^2\), for every \(x\).

| Formula | Meaning | Source

| \(E[y_i|x_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) | Mean response: the mean of \(y\) for a given \(x\). | L2 p.14–16 · L02 unit 02

| \(\beta_0=E[y_i|x_i=0]\) | Intercept: the mean of \(y\) when \(x=0\). | L2 p.16

| \(\beta_1=E[y|x=x^*+1]-E[y|x=x^*]\) | Slope: the difference in mean \(y\) between two populations whose \(x\) values differ by one unit. | L2 p.16, L3 p.5 · L02 unit 02

| \(S(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2\) | Sum of squares. Least squares (LS) picks the \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) that make it a minimum. | L2 p.21 · L02 unit 03

| \(\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_0}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-1)=0\)
\(\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_1}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-x_i)=0\) | The two equations that LS solves (normal equations). | L2 p.23, p.25 · L02 unit 03

| \(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\dfrac{\sum y_ix_i-n\bar y\bar x}{\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2}\) | LS slope estimate. An estimator is a rule that gives an estimate of a parameter from the data. | L2 p.25–26 · L02 unit 03

| \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\) | LS intercept estimate. Thus the fitted line goes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\). | L2 p.23, p.26 · L02 unit 03

| \(L(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2|y)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^n\dfrac{(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\) | Likelihood: the joint density of the data, as a function of the parameters. | L2 p.27 · L02 unit 04

| \(\ell=-\frac n2\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log(\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2\) | Log-likelihood. The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) makes \(\ell\) a maximum. | L2 p.27 · L02 unit 04

| \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_0}=\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])\), \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_1}=\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])x_i\) | Set to 0, these are the LS equations. Thus \(\hat\beta_0^{LS}=\hat\beta_0^{ML}\) and \(\hat\beta_1^{LS}=\hat\beta_1^{ML}\). | L2 p.29 · L02 unit 04

| OLS = MLE under normality | LS needs no normality. LS equals the MLE only when the errors are normal. | L3 p.7 · L03 unit 01

| \(\hat y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i\) | Fitted value: the height of the fitted line at \(x_i\). | L2 p.31 · L02 unit 05

| \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i=y_i-(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i)\) | Residual: observed minus fitted. We can calculate it. | L2 p.31 · L02 unit 05

| \(\epsilon_i=y_i-(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\) | Error: uses the true \(\beta\)'s. We cannot observe it. \(e_i\ne\epsilon_i\), and they have different properties. | L2 p.31, L3 p.8 · L02 unit 05

| \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i=0\), \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_ie_i=0\) | Residual properties. They are the two LS equations at \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\). Added | from L2 p.23, p.25 · L03 unit 01

| \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma^2}=-\dfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\dfrac{1}{2(\sigma^2)^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2\) | Set to 0 to get the MLE of \(\sigma^2\). | L2 p.29, p.35 · L02 unit 06

| \(\hat\sigma^2_{ML}=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2}{n}\) | MLE of \(\sigma^2\). It is biased: its mean is not \(\sigma^2\). | L2 p.35 · L02 unit 06

| \(\hat\sigma^2=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2}{n-2}\), \(E[\hat\sigma^2]=\sigma^2\) | The estimator that the course uses. Unbiased: its mean is \(\sigma^2\). The difference from the MLE is small when \(n\ge50\). | L2 p.35–36 · L02 unit 06

| \(\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2\sim\chi^2_{(n-2)}\) | With \(E[\chi^2_\nu]=\nu\), this gives \(E[\hat\sigma^2]=\sigma^2\). | L2 p.36 · L02 unit 06

| \(\dfrac{\hat\sigma}{\hat\sigma_{MLE}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{n-2}}\) | From the two formulas above (L4 Practice Q1(b)). For \(n=20\): \(\sqrt{20/18}=1.0541\). Added | L04 unit 06

### 01.4 Lecture 3: distribution of the estimates, CI, and tests Slides L3 p.13–49

This section lists the distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_0\), and the confidence intervals and tests that come from it.

Sampling distribution: the distribution of an estimate over many samples of the same size. Unbiased: the mean of the estimator is equal to the parameter.

| Formula | Meaning | Source

| \(\hat\beta_1=\sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i,\quad w_i=\dfrac{x_i-\bar x}{\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2}\) | \(\hat\beta_1\) is a linear combination of independent normals. The \(w_i\) are fixed (they do not depend on \(y\)). | L3 p.12–13 · L03 unit 02

| \(\sum w_i=0,\ \sum w_ix_i=1,\ \sum w_i^2=\dfrac1{S_{xx}}\) | The three facts that the derivations use. Added | from L3 p.18, p.23

| \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\sum w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)=\beta_1\) | \(\hat\beta_1\) is unbiased. | L3 p.14–18 · L03 unit 02

| \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2\sum w_i^2=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\) | A large spread of \(x\) (large \(S_{xx}\)) gives a more precise slope. | L3 p.19–23 · L03 unit 03

| \(\hat\beta_1\sim N\left(\beta_1,\dfrac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\) | Distribution of the slope estimate. | L3 p.24 · L03 unit 03

| \(\hat\beta_0\sim N\left(\beta_0,\ \sigma^2\left(\dfrac1n+\dfrac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\right)\) | Distribution of the intercept estimate. | L3 p.24 · L03 unit 03

| \(\mathrm{cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1)=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1n w_i=0\) | The key step for \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_0)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu_0)\) (Assignment 1 Q1(a)). Added | L03 unit 03

| \(\mathrm{SD}(\hat\beta_1)=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\), \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\dfrac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) | Standard error (SE): the estimated standard deviation of an estimate. Put \(\hat\sigma\) in place of \(\sigma\). | L3 p.35 · L03 unit 04

| \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{\dfrac1n+\dfrac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}}\) | Standard error of the intercept. | L3 p.36 · L03 unit 05

| \(Z=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim N(0,1)\) | Standardized slope (\(\sigma\) known). | L3 p.26 · L03 unit 04

| \(\hat\beta_1\pm1.96\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) | 95% CI when \(\sigma\) is known. | L3 p.27–30 · L03 unit 04

| \(V=\dfrac1{\sigma^2}\sum e_i^2\sim\chi^2_{(n-2)}\); \(Z\) and \(V\) independent | The two parts of the t ratio. | L3 p.33 · L03 unit 04

| \(T=\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-2)}}=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\) | Pivot: a function of data and parameter whose distribution has no unknown values. df \(=n-2\) because we estimate two parameters, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). | L3 p.31–33 · L03 unit 04

| \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) | \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\beta_1\). A confidence interval (CI) is an interval from the data that covers the parameter with the stated probability, before we see the data. | L3 p.34–35, L4 p.5 · L03 unit 04

| \(\hat\beta_0\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)\) | \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\beta_0\). | L3 p.36 · L03 unit 05

| Interpretation of a 95% CI | If we repeat the sampling and make the interval in the same way, 95% of the intervals cover the true \(\beta_1\). It does **not** mean \(P(0.235\le\beta_1\le0.287)=95\%\). | L3 p.38–39 · L03 unit 05

| \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\) vs. \(H_1:\beta_1\ne\theta_0\)
\(t=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\), under \(H_0\): \(T\sim t_{(n-2)}\) | Hypothesis test. The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is the claim that we test. \(t\) is the observed test statistic. Use \(\theta_0=0\) to test "no linear relation". | L3 p.41 · L03 unit 06

| \(p=P(|T|\ge|t|)=2P(T\ge|t|)=2[1-P(T\le|t|)]\) | Two-sided p-value: the probability, under \(H_0\), of a statistic as extreme as the observed one or more extreme. L3 p.42 prints \(2[1-P(T\le-|t|)]\). That is a typing error. | L3 p.42 · L03 unit 06

| Reject \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\) when \(p<\alpha\), that is, when \(|t|>t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\) | The two rules give the same decision. Added | L03 unit 06

| \(p>0.05\): do not reject | We do not "accept" \(H_0\). We only do not have enough evidence to reject it. | L3 p.44 · L03 unit 06

| Meaning of \(p\) | \(p\) is not \(P(\beta_1=0)\). If we reject when \(p<5\%\) and \(H_0\) is true, we reject in error 5% of the time. This rate is the Type I error rate. | L3 p.47 · L03 unit 06

| \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\): \(p=P(T\ge t)\)
\(H_1:\beta_1<\theta_0\): \(p=P(T\le t)\) | One-sided test: the p-value uses only one tail, in the direction of \(H_1\). Reject at level \(\alpha\) when \(t>t_{1-\alpha,n-2}\) (or \(t<-t_{1-\alpha,n-2}\)). The second line and the rejection rule are Added. | L3 p.48–49 · L03 unit 06

### 01.5 Lecture 4: mean response and prediction Slides L4 p.13–39

This section lists the estimate of the mean of \(y\) at a given \(x_0\), its CI, and the prediction interval for one new \(y\).

| Formula | Meaning | Source

| \(\mu_0:=E[y_0|x_0]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_0\) | Mean response at \(x_0\): the mean of \(y\) over all units with \(x=x_0\). | L4 p.13 · L04 unit 02

| \(\hat\mu_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0=\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)\) | Point estimate of \(\mu_0\). At \(x_0=\bar x\), \(\hat\mu_0=\bar y\). | L4 p.13 · L04 unit 02

| \(y_{new}=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new}+\epsilon_{new}\) | One new response at a fixed \(x_{new}\). It has its own error \(\epsilon_{new}\). | L4 p.30 · L04 unit 04

| \(\hat y_{new}\pm t_{1-\frac\alpha2,n-2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+\dfrac1n+\dfrac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\) | \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval (PI): an interval that covers one new response \(y_{new}\) with probability \(1-\alpha\). | L4 p.37 · L04 unit 04

Why the PI is wider than the CI · L4 p.25, p.39

The CI for \(\mu_0\) shows only the uncertainty in the fitted line. The PI also contains the error \(\epsilon_{new}\) of the new unit. This error adds the term \(1\) (that is, \(\sigma^2\)) under the square root. Thus the PI is much wider than the CI. Both are wider at the edges of the data, so the PI lines are not parallel (L4 p.39).

When \(n\) increases, \(\frac1n\) and \(\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\) become small, and the CI width goes to 0. The term 1 stays, so the PI width does not go to 0. Added

Do not trust a CI or a PI at an \(x\) value outside the range of the observed \(x_i\) (extrapolation). The data cannot show that the line is still linear there (Tutorial 2 Q1(f), Assignment 1 Q3(e)). Added

### 01.8 Quantiles: notation and how the exam gives them Added

This section explains the quantile notation and how to change a given probability into the quantile that you need.

Quantile: \(t_{q,\nu}\) is the value with area \(q\) to its left under the \(t_\nu\) density, so \(P(T\le t_{q,\nu})=q\). The same rule gives \(z_q\) for \(N(0,1)\).

A two-sided \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI or PI uses \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\). A one-sided test at level \(\alpha\) uses \(t_{1-\alpha,n-2}\).

| Use | \(\alpha\) | Quantile | \(z\) value

| 90% two-sided, or one-sided at 5% | 0.10 | \(z_{0.95}\) | 1.6449

| 95% two-sided | 0.05 | \(z_{0.975}\) | 1.9600

| 99% two-sided | 0.01 | \(z_{0.995}\) | 2.5758

With a large df, the t quantile is close to the z quantile. For example, \(t_{0.975,234}=1.9702\) and \(z_{0.975}=1.9600\).

| \(P(T>-1.86)=0.95\) | symmetry: \(P(T<1.86)=0.95\) | \(t_{0.95,8}=1.86\) | 90% two-sided CI or PI; one-sided test at 5%

| \(P(T<2.31)=0.975\) | already a left area | \(t_{0.975,8}=2.31\) | 95% two-sided CI or PI; two-sided test at 5%

Change a given probability into a quantile

- Write the statement as a left area \(P(T\le c)=q\). Use \(P(T>c)=1-P(T\le c)\) and the symmetry \(P(T>-c)=P(T<c)\).
- For \(P(|T|>c)=a\), each tail has area \(a/2\). Thus \(P(T\le c)=1-a/2\).
- Read the result as \(c=t_{q,\nu}\).
- Match \(q\) to the question: \(q=1-\alpha/2\) for two-sided, \(q=1-\alpha\) for one-sided.

### 02.1 The model and the four assumptions Slides L2 p.13, p.18

This section gives the model that all derivations in this unit use.

Simple Linear Regression · Lecture 2 · p.13, p.18 \[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i,\quad \epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\qquad\text{Or:}\quad y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\] • \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2\): fixed, unknown parameters • \(\epsilon_i\) is unobserved random error term • Treat \(x_i\) as fixed
Assumptions: 1. Linearity 2. Independence 3. Normality 4. Equal variance (homoskedasticity)

Parameter: a fixed number in the model that we do not know. Estimator: a formula that uses the data to calculate a value for a parameter. A hat (\(\hat\beta_1\)) shows an estimator.

Linearity: the mean of \(y_i\) is \(E[y_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\). Equivalently, \(E[\epsilon_i]=0\).

Independence: the value of one \(y_i\) gives no information about a different \(y_j\).

Equal variance: \(\mathrm{Var}[y_i]=\sigma^2\) is the same for all \(i\). Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean.

Normality: each \(y_i\) (each \(\epsilon_i\)) has a Normal distribution.

Because the \(x_i\) are fixed, every function of the \(x_i\) alone (\(\bar x\), \(S_{xx}\), \(w_i\)) is a constant. The derivations use this fact many times.

### 02.2 Least squares: the normal equations and the solution Slides L2 p.21–26

**Result.** The least squares estimators are \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\).

Least squares (LS): choose \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) that make the sum of squares \(S(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2\) as small as possible.

\(S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2\) and \(S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)(x_i-\bar x)\) (Lecture 2 · p.7, p.26).

Normal equations Added: the two equations that we get when we set the two partial derivatives of \(S\) to 0. (The slides do not use this name.)

1

\[\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_0}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-1)\]

Chain rule.

2

\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i)\]

Set to 0. Divide by \(-2\). This is normal equation 1.

3

\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i-n\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\]

4

\[\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\]

Divide by \(n\).

5

\[\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_1}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-x_i)\]

Chain rule.

6

\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n y_ix_i-\hat\beta_0n\bar x-\hat\beta_1\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\]

Set to 0. Divide by \(-2\). Use \(\sum x_i=n\bar x\). This is normal equation 2.

7

\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n y_ix_i-(\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x)n\bar x-\hat\beta_1\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\]

Substitute line 4.

8

\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n y_ix_i-n\bar y\bar x+\hat\beta_1\Big(n\bar x^2-\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\Big)\]

9

\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n y_ix_i-n\bar y\bar x}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2-n\bar x^2}\]

10

\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum y_i(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum x_i(x_i-\bar x)}=\frac{\sum(y_i-\bar y)(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\]

Because \(\bar y\sum(x_i-\bar x)=\bar y(\sum x_i-n\bar x)=0\) (Lecture 2 · p.26). The same step with \(\bar x\) changes the denominator.

These formulas use no model assumption: they are a calculation on the data. The slides say: "OLS well defined even without normality" (Lecture 3 · p.7). Section 02.3 shows that the MLE gives the same formulas.

### 02.3 The MLE of \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) is equal to LS; the MLE of \(\sigma^2\) Slides L2 p.27–29, p.34

**Result.** Under the Normal model, \(\hat\beta_0^{ML}=\hat\beta_0^{LS}\), \(\hat\beta_1^{ML}=\hat\beta_1^{LS}\), and \(\hat\sigma^2_{ML}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2\).

Likelihood \(L\): the joint density of the observed \(y_1,\dots,y_n\), as a function of the parameters. Log-likelihood \(\ell\): the natural log of \(L\).

Maximum likelihood estimator (MLE): the parameter values that make the likelihood as large as possible.

Fitted value \(\hat y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i\). Residual \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\): the observed value minus the fitted value (Lecture 2 · p.31).

Part 1: \(\beta_0,\beta_1\).

1

\[L(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2|y)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\]

\(y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\). Independence: the joint density is the product of the densities.

2

\[=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\Big(-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\]

3

\[\ell=-\frac n2\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log(\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])^2\]

Take the log.

4

\[\ell=-\frac n2\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log(\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}S(\beta_0,\beta_1)\]

The sum is the LS sum of squares \(S\).

5

\[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_0}=\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_0}\]

Slides p.28 (\(\star\)). In the same way, \(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_1}=\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i])x_i=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_1}\) (\(\star\star\)).

\[\hat\beta_0^{ML}=\hat\beta_0^{LS},\qquad\hat\beta_1^{ML}=\hat\beta_1^{LS}\]

Setting (\(\star\)) and (\(\star\star\)) to 0 gives the two normal equations of 02.2. Thus it "is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squares" (Slides p.29).

1

### 02.4 Why \(\hat\sigma^2\) divides by \(n-2\) Slides L2 p.36; L3 p.8

**Result.** \(\hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\). \(\hat\sigma^2_{ML}\) is not.

### 02.9 The distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) and the \(T\) statistic Slides L3 p.13, p.24, p.26, p.31–35

**Result.** \(T=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\). This gives the confidence interval and the test for \(\beta_1\).

\(t_\nu\) distribution: if \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\) are independent, then \(Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}\sim t_\nu\) (Lecture 3 · p.31).

Pivot Added: a quantity that contains the parameter and has a distribution with no unknown parameters. \(T\) is a pivot. (The slides do not use this name.)

Standard error: the estimated standard deviation of an estimator. \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) (Lecture 3 · p.35).

\[\hat\beta_1=\sum_{i=1}^nw_iy_i,\qquad y_i\overset{indep}{\sim}N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\]

Mean from 02.6. Variance from 02.7 (Slides p.24).

Subtract the mean. Divide by the standard deviation (Slides p.26).

\[V=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^ne_i^2\sim\chi^2_{(n-2)},\quad Z\text{ and }V\text{ independent}\]

Given on Slides p.31 ("It can be shown"). Do not prove it.

\[\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\]

From \(T\) to the confidence interval (Slides p.34–35). A quantile \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\) is the value with probability \(1-\alpha/2\) below it in the \(t_{(n-2)}\) distribution. \[1-\alpha=P\Big(-t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\le\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\le t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\Big)\]

\[=P\Big(\hat\beta_1-t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\le\beta_1\le\hat\beta_1+t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\Big)\]

Multiply by \(\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\). Subtract \(\hat\beta_1\). Multiply by \(-1\) (the inequalities change direction).

\[\text{CI for }\beta_1:\quad\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\]

Test of \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\): under \(H_0\), \(T=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\). Two-sided p-value: \(P(|T|\ge|t|)=2P(T\ge|t|)\) (Slides p.41–42).

Answer "If an assumption does not hold, is a result still true?"

- Write the derivation of the result in short form.
- Find each line that uses the assumption.
- If no line uses the assumption, the result is still true. Say so and name the lines that the derivation uses.
- If a line uses the assumption, that line can fail. Then the result is not guaranteed.
- For an interval or a test, say what fails: the SE, or the \(t_{(n-2)}\) distribution, or the coverage.

**Self-check:** Your answer names a specific line or formula, not only the name of the assumption.

Model answer · A1 Q1(b): if normality does not hold, is \(\hat\beta_0\) unbiased?

Yes. The derivation is \(E[\hat\beta_0]=E[\bar y]-\bar x\,E[\hat\beta_1]=\frac1n\sum(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)-\bar x\beta_1=\beta_0\).

It uses linearity of expectation, the fixed \(x_i\), and \(E[y_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) (linearity assumption). \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\beta_1\) uses the same facts and W1, W2.

No line uses the Normal distribution. Thus \(\hat\beta_0\) is unbiased when the linearity assumption holds, also without normality.

Model answer · A1 Q1(c): if independence does not hold, is the usual CI for \(\beta_1\) valid?

No. The usual CI is \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\).

Its width uses \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\). That derivation uses \(\mathrm{Var}[\sum w_iy_i]=\sum w_i^2\mathrm{Var}[y_i]\), which needs independent \(y_i\).

Without independence, \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]\) is in general not equal to \(\sigma^2/S_{xx}\). Thus the SE is not correct.

Also, the result \(T\sim t_{(n-2)}\) needs independent Normal \(y_i\) (Slides L3 p.13, p.31). Thus the interval does not have the stated \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) coverage in general.

The estimate \(\hat\beta_1\) is still unbiased, because \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\beta_1\) uses only linearity.

\(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]\), \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{A}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{A}^T\), \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}+c,\ \mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y}+d)=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{b}\).

If \(\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{d}\), then \(\mathbf{u}\sim MVN(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{d},\ \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{C}^T)\).

Matrix calculus Slides L5 p.20–21

\(\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{a}\), \(\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{y}\), which is \(2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\) when \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric.

### 03.2 Derivation: \(E[\hat\mu_0]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]\) Slides L4 p.10–18

This section derives the mean, the variance and the distribution of \(\hat\mu_0\). The exam can ask for this derivation in full.

Lecture 4 · p.10–12 For an arbitrary \(x_0\), the mean outcome is \(\mu_0:=E[y_0\mid x_0]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_0\). We can estimate this as \[\hat\mu_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0=\left(\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\right)+\hat\beta_1x_0=\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)\]

The mean response \(\mu_0\) is the average value of \(y\) for all units with covariate value \(x_0\). It is the height of the true line at \(x_0\).

An estimator is a formula that we calculate from random data, so it is a random variable. \(\hat\mu_0\) is the estimator of \(\mu_0\).

An estimator is unbiased when its expectation (its long-run average) equals the true value.

The variance of a random variable is its expected squared distance from its expectation.

**Mean** Slides L4 p.14–15

1

\[E[\hat\mu_0]=E[\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0]\]

2

\[=E[\hat\beta_0]+E[\hat\beta_1]\,x_0\]

\(x_0\) is a fixed number.

3

\[=\beta_0+\beta_1x_0=\mu_0\]

Lecture 3: \(E[\hat\beta_0]=\beta_0\) and \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\beta_1\).

Thus \(\hat\mu_0\) is unbiased. This step uses only \(E[\epsilon_i]=0\) and the linear form of the model. It does not use normality.

**Variance · the slide route** Slides L4 p.16–17

The slide route writes \(\hat\mu_0\) as one sum \(\sum c_iy_i\) with constants \(c_i\). Then it uses the rule for independent \(y_i\). The route uses \(\hat\beta_1=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{x_i-\bar x}{S_{xx}}y_i\) (Lecture 3) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=0\).

1

\[\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]=\mathrm{Var}[\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)]\]

2

\[=\mathrm{Var}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1n y_i+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{x_i-\bar x}{S_{xx}}y_i\,(x_0-\bar x)\right]\]

Substitute \(\bar y\) and \(\hat\beta_1\).

3

\[=\mathrm{Var}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_i-\bar x)(x_0-\bar x)}{S_{xx}}\right)y_i\right]\]

4

\[=\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_i-\bar x)(x_0-\bar x)}{S_{xx}}\right)^2\sigma^2\]

The \(y_i\) are independent with \(\mathrm{Var}[y_i]=\sigma^2\). Each constant comes out squared.

5

\[=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac1{n^2}+\frac{(x_i-\bar x)^2(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}^2}+\frac2n\,\frac{(x_i-\bar x)(x_0-\bar x)}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

6

\[=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}^2}\,S_{xx}+\frac2n\,\frac{x_0-\bar x}{S_{xx}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)\right)\]

\(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2=S_{xx}\).

7

\[=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

The cross term is 0, because \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=0\).

Variance · the covariance route (same result) · Added, uses Slides L2 p.5–6 and L5 p.26

The cross term in line 5 is the covariance term. A covariance \(\mathrm{cov}(U,V)\) measures how two random variables change together. This route shows the covariance step directly.

1

\[\mathrm{Var}[\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)]=\mathrm{Var}[\bar y]+(x_0-\bar x)^2\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]+2(x_0-\bar x)\,\mathrm{cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1)\]

\(\mathrm{Var}(U+V)=\mathrm{cov}(U+V,U+V)\). Expand with the rule \(\mathrm{cov}(U+V,X+Y)=\dots\) (L2 p.6).

2

\[\mathrm{cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\frac1n\,\frac{x_j-\bar x}{S_{xx}}\,\mathrm{cov}(y_i,y_j)\]

L5 p.26: \(\mathrm{cov}(\sum a_iy_i,\sum b_jy_j)=\sum_i\sum_j a_ib_j\,\mathrm{cov}(y_i,y_j)\).

3

\[=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1n\,\frac{x_i-\bar x}{S_{xx}}\,\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{nS_{xx}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=0\]

Independence: \(\mathrm{cov}(y_i,y_j)=0\) for \(i\ne j\), and \(\mathrm{cov}(y_i,y_i)=\sigma^2\).

4

\[\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]=\frac{\sigma^2}n+(x_0-\bar x)^2\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}+0=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

\(\mathrm{Var}[\bar y]=\sigma^2/n\). Lecture 3: \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\).

**Distribution** Slides L4 p.18

Lecture 4 · p.18 \[\Longrightarrow\ \hat\mu_0\sim N\left[\mu_0,\ \sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}\right)\right]\quad\text{(why?)}\]

The answer to "why?": line 3 shows that \(\hat\mu_0=\sum c_iy_i\) with constants \(c_i\). The \(y_i\) are independent normal variables. A linear combination of independent normal variables is normal (Slides L3 p.12–13). The mean and the variance come from the two chains above.

Result: \(\hat\mu_0\) is unbiased and normal, with the smallest variance \(\sigma^2/n\) at \(x_0=\bar x\). Section 03.3 standardizes it to make a CI. Full version: L04 unit 02.

### 03.3 The confidence interval for the mean response Slides L4 p.19–20

This section changes the distribution of \(\hat\mu_0\) into a confidence interval for \(\mu_0\).

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval (CI) is a random interval that contains the fixed true value in \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of repeated samples.

The quantile \(t_{q,\,n-2}\) is the number with area \(q\) to its left under the \(t_{n-2}\) density.

1

\[\frac{\hat\mu_0-\mu_0}{\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\sim N(0,1)\]

Subtract the mean. Divide by the standard deviation (03.2).

2

\[\frac{\hat\mu_0-\mu_0}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\sim t_{n-2}\]

L4 p.19: "by the same logic as before" (L3 p.31–33). Replace \(\sigma\) with \(\hat\sigma\). Then \(Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}\sim t_\nu\) with \(\nu=n-2\).

3

\[1-\alpha=P\left(-t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\le\frac{\hat\mu_0-\mu_0}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\le t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\right)\]

4

\[\hat\mu_0\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

Solve the two inequalities for \(\mu_0\).

How to calculate the CI for \(\mu_0\)

- Calculate \(\hat\mu_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0\).
- Calculate the factor \(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\).
- Multiply its square root by \(\hat\sigma\). This is the standard error of \(\hat\mu_0\).
- Multiply by the quantile \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\) that the question gives.
- Write \(\hat\mu_0\) plus or minus this half-width.

**Self-check:** ① Use \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. ② The factor is at least \(1/n\), with equality only at \(x_0=\bar x\). ③ The interval is centered at \(\hat\mu_0\).

Result: the CI for \(\mu_0\) has the half-width \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1/n+(x_0-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}}\). Section 03.4 adds one term to get the PI. Full version: L04 unit 03.

### 03.4 Derivation: the prediction error and the prediction interval Slides L4 p.27–37

This section derives the mean, the variance and the distribution of the prediction error. Then it builds the prediction interval.

Lecture 4 · p.27–31 Instead of the mean, we might want to predict the response itself for an observation with covariate \(x_{new}\) (as always treating \(x_{new}\) as fixed): \[y_{new}=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new}+\epsilon_{new}\] Define the predicted value \(\hat y_{new}=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_{new}\). Define the prediction error: \(\hat y_{new}-y_{new}\).

\(y_{new}\) is one new observation. It is a random variable, not a parameter. Its error \(\epsilon_{new}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\) is independent of \(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_n\).

The prediction error is the difference between the predicted value and the new observation.

\(\hat y_{new}\) has the same formula as \(\hat\mu_0\) with \(x_0=x_{new}\). The difference is the target: \(\hat\mu_0\) estimates a fixed mean, and \(\hat y_{new}\) predicts a random value.

**Mean** Slides L4 p.32–33

1

\[E[\hat y_{new}-y_{new}]=E[(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_{new})-(\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new}+\epsilon_{new})]\]

2

\[=E[\hat\beta_0]+E[\hat\beta_1]x_{new}-\beta_0-\beta_1x_{new}-E[\epsilon_{new}]\]

3

\[=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new}-(\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new})-0=0\]

Unbiased \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\) (L3), and \(E[\epsilon_{new}]=0\).

**Variance** Slides L4 p.33–35

"Now note that \(\hat y_{new}\) and \(y_{new}\) are independent (why?)". Answer: \(\hat y_{new}\) uses only \(y_1,\dots,y_n\). The random part of \(y_{new}\) is \(\epsilon_{new}\), and \(\epsilon_{new}\) is independent of \(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_n\). Two random variables are independent when the value of one does not change the distribution of the other.

1

\[\mathrm{Var}[\hat y_{new}-y_{new}]=\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_{new}]+\mathrm{Var}[y_{new}]\]

Independence, so the covariance term is 0. \(\mathrm{Var}[-y_{new}]=(-1)^2\mathrm{Var}[y_{new}]\).

2

\[=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)+\sigma^2\]

03.2 with \(x_0=x_{new}\). \(\mathrm{Var}[y_{new}]=\mathrm{Var}[\epsilon_{new}]=\sigma^2\).

3

\[=\sigma^2\left(1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

**Distribution and interval** Slides L4 p.36–37

\(\hat y_{new}-y_{new}\) is a linear combination of the independent normal variables \(y_1,\dots,y_n,\epsilon_{new}\). Thus it is normal.

1

\[\hat y_{new}-y_{new}\sim N\left[0,\ \sigma^2\left(1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\right]\]

2

\[\frac{\hat y_{new}-y_{new}}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\sim t_{n-2}\]

Standardize. Then replace \(\sigma\) with \(\hat\sigma\), as in 03.3.

3

\[\hat y_{new}\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

Solve \(-t\le\cdot\le t\) for \(y_{new}\) (L4 p.37).

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval (PI) is a random interval that contains the new random observation \(y_{new}\) with probability \(1-\alpha\).

Result: the PI has the same center as the CI. The only change is the extra "1" inside the square root. Full version: L04 unit 04.

### 03.5 Why the PI is wider, and what happens far from \(\bar x\) Slides L4 p.22–25, p.39 · Added

This section explains the two shape facts that the exam asks in words. Then a numeric example and a figure show them.

Why the PI is wider than the CI Slides L4 p.25, p.39

The CI has one source of error: the error in the estimated line, \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\).

The PI has two sources: the same line error, plus the new error \(\epsilon_{new}\) with variance \(\sigma^2\).

The slides say: "even if we got the mean absolutely perfect—the new points wouldn't fall directly on this line!"

What happens far from \(\bar x\) Slides L4 p.22–23, p.39

Both intervals contain \((x-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}\). This term is 0 at \(\bar x\) and increases with the distance from \(\bar x\).

Thus both intervals are narrowest at \(x=\bar x\) and become wider toward the edges. An error in \(\hat\beta_1\) moves the fitted line more at points far from \(\bar x\).

The PI bands are "not parallel (slightly wider at the edges)" (L4 p.39). The constant "1" is large, so the PI changes less in relative terms.

Three exact facts Added

① (PI half-width)\(^2-\)(CI half-width)\(^2=t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}^2\,\hat\sigma^2\) at every \(x\). The gap comes only from \(\epsilon_{new}\).

② At \(x=\bar x\): PI half-width / CI half-width \(=\sqrt{1+1/n}\,/\sqrt{1/n}=\sqrt{n+1}\).

③ When \(n\) increases and \(S_{xx}\) increases with it, \(1/n\to0\) and \((x-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}\to0\). The CI half-width goes to 0. The PI half-width does not go to 0; with a large \(n\) it is near \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma\).

Extrapolation Added · HW1 Q3(e), Tutorial 2 Q1(f)

Extrapolation is prediction at an \(x\) value outside the range of the observed \(x_i\).

There, the interval is wide. Also, no data show that the line is still linear there. Thus the formulas can be wrong, and we must not trust the interval.

**Example · L4 Practice Q2 numbers** Added · data from Slides L4 p.43

This example calculates both 95% intervals at \(x=0\) and at \(x=5\). It shows the two shape facts with numbers.

\[t_{0.975,8}\,\hat\sigma=2.306\times0.4=0.9224\]

\[\text{CI: }0.9224\sqrt{0.1+0}=0.9224\times0.3162=0.2917\ \Rightarrow\ (-2.292,\ -1.708)\]

### 03.7 The regression model in matrix form Slides L5 p.7–8, p.45

This section writes the model with vectors and matrices and checks the dimension of each one.

Lecture 5 · p.8, p.45 \[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_px_{ip}+\epsilon_i,\qquad \epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\] \[\left[\begin{array}{c}y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}1&x_{11}&x_{12}&\cdots&x_{1p}\\1&x_{21}&x_{22}&\cdots&x_{2p}\\\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\1&x_{n1}&x_{n2}&\cdots&x_{np}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_p\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{c}\epsilon_1\\\epsilon_2\\\vdots\\\epsilon_n\end{array}\right]\] Or more simply: \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\epsilon,\ \boldsymbol\epsilon\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\iff\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta,\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\)

Multiple linear regression (MLR) is the model with \(p\) covariates \(x_{i1},\dots,x_{ip}\). SLR is the case \(p=1\).

The design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) has one row for each observation. Its first column is all 1s, for the intercept \(\beta_0\).

\(\mathbf{I}\) is the identity matrix: 1 on the diagonal and 0 in every other place.

Dimensions (rows × columns)

\(\mathbf{y}\): \(n\times1\). \(\mathbf{X}\): \(n\times(p+1)\). \(\boldsymbol\beta\): \((p+1)\times1\). \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\): \(n\times1\). \(\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\): \(n\times n\).

Check: \([n\times(p+1)]\,[(p+1)\times1]=n\times1\). This matches \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\).

SLR (\(p=1\))

\(\mathbf{X}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}1&x_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n\end{array}\right]\) is \(n\times2\), and \(\boldsymbol\beta=\left[\begin{array}{c}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{array}\right]\) is \(2\times1\). Row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\) is \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\).

How to read \(\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\): each diagonal entry \(\sigma^2\) is \(\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i)\) (equal variance). Each off-diagonal 0 is \(\mathrm{cov}(\epsilon_i,\epsilon_j)\). For an MVN vector, zero covariance gives independence (03.11). Thus \(MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\) is the same statement as \(\epsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)\).

Result: \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\epsilon\) states all four assumptions in one line. Full version: L05 unit 01.

### 03.8 Transpose, inverse and trace rules Slides L5 p.9–19

This section lists the eleven matrix rules on the slides and uses them in two short proofs.

Lecture 5 · p.9–19 • \([\mathbf{C}^T]_{ij}=[\mathbf{C}]_{ji}\) • \(\mathbf{C}\) is symmetric if \(\mathbf{C}^T=\mathbf{C}\) • \((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T=\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T\)
• If square matrix (i.e. \(n\times n\)) \(\mathbf{B}\) is nonsingular, then \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}\)
• \((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\), if both square and nonsingular • \((\mathbf{A}^T)^{-1}=(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^T\)
• \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})=\sum_j a_{jj}\) for \(n\times n\) matrix • \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})+\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})\) • \(\mathrm{tr}(c\mathbf{A})=c\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})\)
• \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}^T)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})\) • \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\)

The transpose \(\mathbf{C}^T\) changes the rows of \(\mathbf{C}\) into columns.

A square matrix is nonsingular when it has an inverse \(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\), the matrix that gives \(\mathbf{I}\) when you multiply it with \(\mathbf{B}\).

The trace \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})\) of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries.

Product rules reverse the order: \((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T=\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T\) and \((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\).

### 03.9 Matrix calculus: two rules and their use Slides L5 p.20–21

This section gives the derivative of a scalar with respect to a vector. Then it checks the quadratic rule by hand and uses it to find a stationary point.

Lecture 5 · p.20–21 Let \(z=f(y_1,\dots,y_k)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=\left[\begin{array}{c}y_1\\\vdots\\y_k\end{array}\right]\), then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\left[\begin{array}{c}\partial z/\partial y_1\\\vdots\\\partial z/\partial y_k\end{array}\right]\).
• If \(z=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}\), where \(\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_k)^T\) is a vector, then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{a}\)
• If \(z=\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\) where \(\mathbf{A}\) is a \(k\times k\) matrix, then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{y}\), and if \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric, then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\)

\(\partial z/\partial\mathbf{y}\) is a \(k\times1\) vector. Entry \(i\) is the partial derivative of the scalar \(z\) with respect to \(y_i\).

A quadratic form \(\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}=\sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}y_iy_j\) is a scalar with only second-degree terms in the \(y_i\).

### 03.10 Derivation: \(E\) and \(\mathrm{Var}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}\) Slides L5 p.22–35

This section defines the mean vector and the covariance matrix. Then it derives the rules for a linear transformation and uses them on a \(2\times2\) example.

Lecture 5 · p.22–29 The mean of \(\mathbf{y}\) is \(E[\mathbf{y}]=(E[y_1],\dots,E[y_n])^T\). The variance is \(\mathrm{var}(\mathbf{y})=E[(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^T]\), with \([\mathbf{V}]_{ii}=\mathrm{var}(y_i)\) and \([\mathbf{V}]_{ij}=\mathrm{cov}(y_i,y_j)\).
• \(\mathbf{V}\) is symmetric: \(\mathbf{V}_{ij}=\mathbf{V}_{ji}\) • \(\mathbf{V}\) is positive semi-definite: for all \(\mathbf{a}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), \(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{a}\ge0\)
• \(E[\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}+b]=\mathbf{a}^TE[\mathbf{y}]+b\) • \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{a}^T\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{a}\) • \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}+c,\ \mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y}+d)=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{b}\)

A random vector is a column of random variables. Its mean vector \(E[\mathbf{y}]\) holds the mean of each entry.

The covariance matrix \(\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\) holds the variances on the diagonal and the covariances off the diagonal.

Positive semi-definite means \(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{a}\ge0\) for every \(\mathbf{a}\). The reason: \(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{a}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y})\), and a variance is never negative.

**Derivation** Slides L5 p.32–35 · \(+\mathbf{b}\) Added

The slides prove \(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]\) row by row: row \(i\) is \(E[\sum_ja_{ij}y_j]=\sum_ja_{ij}E[y_j]\). A constant vector \(\mathbf{b}\) adds \(E[\mathbf{b}]=\mathbf{b}\), so \(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}]=\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]+\mathbf{b}\). The variance chain follows.

1

\[\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})=E[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}-E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}])(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}-E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}])^T]\]

Definition (L5 p.23).

2

\[=E[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}])(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}])^T]\]

\(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}]=\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]+\mathbf{b}\); the \(\mathbf{b}\) cancels.

3

\[=E[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}])(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}]))^T]\]

4

\[=E[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}])(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}])^T\mathbf{A}^T]\]

\((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T=\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T\).

5

\[=\mathbf{A}\,E[(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}])(\mathbf{y}-E[\mathbf{y}])^T]\,\mathbf{A}^T\]

Constant matrices come out of \(E\) (L5 p.32–33).

6

\[=\mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{A}^T\]

How to calculate \(E\) and \(\mathrm{Var}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}\)

- Write \(\mathbf{A}\) so that row \(i\) holds the coefficients of output \(i\).
- Calculate \(\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]+\mathbf{b}\).
- Calculate \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\) first. Then multiply the result by \(\mathbf{A}^T\).

**Self-check:** ① \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{A}^T\) must be symmetric. ② Its diagonal must be \(\ge0\). ③ Each diagonal entry must equal \(\mathbf{a}_i^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{a}_i\), with \(\mathbf{a}_i^T\) as row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\). ④ \(\mathbf{b}\) changes the mean only.

### 03.11 The multivariate normal distribution Slides L5 p.36–44

This section gives the MVN definition and its four properties. Then it shows why zero covariance gives independence only for an MVN vector, and solves one exercise.

Lecture 5 · p.36 Let \(\mathbf{z}=(z_1,\dots,z_n)^T\) be a random vector of i.i.d standard normal random variables, i.e. \(z_i\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,1)\). Then \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\boldsymbol\mu\) has multivariate normal distribution, i.e.: \(\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) where \(E[\mathbf{y}]=\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})=\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\). \[f(\mathbf{y})=\frac1{(2\pi)^{n/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac12(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^T\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right)\]

A multivariate normal (MVN) vector is a vector of i.i.d. standard normal variables, multiplied by a constant matrix, plus a constant vector.

The mean and the variance come from 03.10: \(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\boldsymbol\mu]=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}+\boldsymbol\mu=\boldsymbol\mu\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\boldsymbol\mu)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{A}^T=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\).

Lecture 5 · p.37–40 · Properties of \(\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) **Linearity:** If \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{d}\), then: \(\mathbf{u}\sim MVN(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{d},\ \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{C}^T)\)
**Marginal Distribution:** If \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=(y_1,\dots,y_m)^T\subset\mathbf{y}\) is a vector subset of \(\mathbf{y}\), then \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\) is MVN-distributed.
**Conditional Distribution:** If \(\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_1^T,\mathbf{y}_2^T)^T\), then \(\mathbf{y}_1\mid\mathbf{y}_2\) is MVN-distributed.
**Independence:** If \(\Sigma_{ij}=0\), then \(y_i\) and \(y_j\) are independent.

A marginal distribution is the distribution of some entries of the vector when we ignore the other entries. Its mean and covariance are the matching entries of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\).

A conditional distribution is the distribution of \(\mathbf{y}_1\) when the value of \(\mathbf{y}_2\) is known.

Linearity is the 03.10 rule plus one more fact: the result is again MVN. The reason: \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{d}=(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{z}+(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{d})\) has the form of the definition.

Zero covariance and independence · Slides L5 p.41–44 + Added

The independence property needs \(\mathbf{y}\) to be MVN. Two normal variables with covariance 0 are not always independent.

Slide exercise: \(X_1\sim N(0,1)\), \(B\sim\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)\) independent of \(X_1\), and \(X_2=X_1\) if \(B=0\), \(X_2=-X_1\) if \(B=1\). Then \(X_2\sim N(0,1)\). But \(Y=X_1+X_2\) has \(P(Y=0)=P(B=1)=1/2\), so \(Y\) is not normal. By linearity, \((X_1,X_2)^T\) cannot be MVN.

Added: \(\mathrm{cov}(X_1,X_2)=E[X_1X_2]=E[X_1^2(1-2B)]=E[X_1^2]\,E[1-2B]=1\times0=0\). But \(|X_2|=|X_1|\), so \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are not independent.

Result: "each entry is normal" is not enough for MVN. Use "zero covariance ⇒ independent" only when you know that the vector is MVN.

### 04.1 Read the coefficient table Slides L3 p.45

This section tells what each cell of the coefficient table is, and how to get a missing cell.

Coefficient table: a table with one row for each coefficient. The columns are the estimate, its standard error, the t value, and the p-value.

**What each cell is** Slides L3 p.35, p.45

| Cell | Slope row (\(\beta_1\)) | Intercept row (\(\beta_0\))

Get a missing cell

- Missing t value: divide the estimate by the standard error.
- Missing standard error: divide the estimate by the t value.
- Missing \(n\): add 2 to the degrees of freedom.
- Missing \(\hat\sigma^2\): square the residual standard error.
- Missing \(S_{xx}\): calculate \((\hat\sigma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1))^2\).

### 04.3 Make and interpret a 95% CI for \(\beta_1\) Slides L3 p.34–39

This section makes a confidence interval from the table. A confidence interval (CI) is a random interval that contains the true parameter with a stated probability over repeated samples.

95% CI for β1 — Unknown σ / Standard Errors · Lecture 3 · p.34–35 Often written \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\), as in: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\)
Can write a CI as \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\)

Interval interpretation · Lecture 3 · p.38–39 Does this mean \(P(0.235\le\beta_1\le0.287)=95\%\)? No!
If we were to draw samples repeatedly and construct intervals in the same way, any given interval may or may not cover the truth. On average, 95% will cover the truth.

\(\beta_1\) is a fixed number. One computed interval contains it or does not contain it. The 95% is a property of the method over many samples. If \(\sigma\) is known, replace \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\) with the \(N(0,1)\) quantile (1.96 for 95%) and \(\hat\sigma\) with \(\sigma\) (Slides L3 p.27–30).

Make a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\beta_1\)

- Read \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) from the slope row.
- Read the df \(=n-2\) from the residual standard error line.
- Get \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\) from the question. For 95%, \(1-\alpha/2=0.975\).
- Calculate the margin \(t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\times\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\).
- Subtract the margin from \(\hat\beta_1\), then add it.
- Write the interval in context. Add the repeated-sampling sentence.

**Self-check:** \(\hat\beta_1\) is at the center of the interval.

### 04.4 Test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) from the table Slides L3 p.41–45

This section answers "Does \(y\) depend on \(x\)?" with the table. \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) says that the mean of \(y\) does not change with \(x\).

Null hypothesis \(H_0\): the claim that we test. Alternative hypothesis \(H_1\): the claim against \(H_0\).

p-value: the probability, under \(H_0\), of a test statistic as extreme as the observed one or more extreme.

Significance level \(\alpha\): the limit for the p-value. When the p-value is less than \(\alpha\), we reject \(H_0\). The default is \(\alpha=0.05\).

Example: Testing a Hypothesis · Lecture 3 · p.43–44 The pval < 0.05. Hence we reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level.
What if pval > 0.05? Would we accept the null? No! Simply would not have enough evidence to reject.

Test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) with the table

- Write \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) and \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0\).
- Write the statistic \(T=\hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\sim t_{(n-2)}\) under \(H_0\).
- Read the observed \(t\) and the p-value from the slope row.
- Compare the p-value with \(\alpha\).
- Write the decision and one sentence in context.

**Self-check:** Use only "reject \(H_0\)" or "not enough evidence to reject \(H_0\)". Never write "accept \(H_0\)".

### 04.6 One-sided p-value from the table's two-sided p-value Slides L3 p.48–49

This section changes the two-sided p-value \(p_2\) of the table into a one-sided p-value. The test statistic does not change.

One-sided hypothesis tests · Lecture 3 · p.48–49 Sometimes we are interested in evidence one direction: e.g., \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\)
• So large values of \(\hat\beta_1-\theta_0\) indicate evidence against the null in favour of \(H_1\)
• \(\Rightarrow\) large values of \(\frac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\) indicate evidence in favour of \(H_1\)
• I.e. p-value only includes one tail

For \(H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0\), the p-value is the right tail \(P(T\ge t)\). For \(H_1:\beta_1<\theta_0\), the p-value is the left tail \(P(T\le t)\). Full teaching: L03 unit 06.

Get the one-sided p-value from \(p_2\) Added

- Find the side of \(H_1\): ">" is the right side, "<" is the left side.
- Look at the sign of \(t\).
- If \(t\) is on the side of \(H_1\), the one-sided p-value is \(p_2/2\).
- If \(t\) is on the other side, the one-sided p-value is \(1-p_2/2\).

**Self-check:** When \(\hat\beta_1\) is on the wrong side of \(\theta_0\) for \(H_1\), the p-value must be more than 0.5. Then we do not reject \(H_0\) at a usual level such as \(\alpha=0.05\).

**Why** Added · for \(t<0\) (the hospital case)

1

\[p_2=2P(T\ge|t|)=2P(T\le-|t|)\]

Slides L3 p.42 and symmetry of the t distribution about 0.

2

\[H_1:\beta_1<\theta_0:\quad P(T\le t)=P(T\le-|t|)=p_2/2\]

\(t<0\), so \(t=-|t|\).

3

\[H_1:\beta_1>\theta_0:\quad P(T\ge t)=1-P(T\le t)=1-p_2/2\]

Complement of line 2.

This section gives the two formulas that you must know, and the words that tell you which one to use.

Lecture 4 · p.10, p.13, p.20 For an arbitrary \(x_0\), the mean outcome is \(\mu_0:=E[y_0\mid x_0]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_0\). We can estimate this as \[\hat\mu_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0=\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)\] and a 100(1−α)% CI is: \[\hat\mu_0\pm t_{1-\frac\alpha2,\,n-2}\;\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}}\]

Lecture 4 · p.28, p.37 \[y_{new}=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{new}+\epsilon_{new},\qquad \hat y_{new}=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_{new}\] So a 100(1−α)% prediction interval is: \[\hat y_{new}\pm t_{1-\frac\alpha2,\,n-2}\;\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_{new}-\bar x)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}}\]

The mean response \(\mu_0\) is the mean of \(y\) for all units with \(x=x_0\). It is a fixed number that we do not know.

A confidence interval (CI) is an interval from the data that contains a fixed unknown parameter for \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of repeated samples.

A prediction interval (PI) is an interval from the data that contains the random response \(y_{new}\) of one new unit for \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of repeated samples.

The quantile \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\) is the point of the \(t_{(n-2)}\) distribution with area \(1-\alpha/2\) on its left side.

The standard error (SE) of \(\hat\mu_0\) is the estimate of its standard deviation: \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu_0)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\), with \(S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2\).

The two centers have the same number: \(\hat\mu_0=\hat y_{new}\) when \(x_0=x_{new}\). Only the "1+" under the square root is different.

The "1+" is from \(\mathrm{Var}[y_{new}]=\sigma^2\), the variance of the new error \(\epsilon_{new}\) (Slides L4 p.35).

Which interval? · Added

| Question wording | Target | Interval

| "estimated mean / average / expected satisfaction for patients with severity 55" | \(\mu_0\), a fixed number | CI, no "1+"

| "predict the satisfaction of a new patient / one patient / this patient with severity 55" | \(y_{new}\), a random value | PI, with "1+"

Key words "mean", "average", "expected" ask for a CI. Key words "new", "a single", "an individual", "predict" ask for a PI.

Calculate a CI for \(\mu_0\) or a PI for \(y_{new}\)

- Read \(\hat\beta_0\), \(\hat\beta_1\), \(\hat\sigma\) and \(n-2\) from the table.
- Calculate the center \(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0\).
- Calculate \((x_0-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}\).
- Add \(1/n\). For a PI, also add 1.
- Take the square root and multiply by \(\hat\sigma\).
- Multiply by \(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\). The result is the half-width.
- Write center ± half-width.

**Self-check:** ① The PI must be wider than the CI at the same \(x_0\). ② The CI half-width must be smallest at \(x_0=\bar x\). ③ The degrees of freedom are \(n-2\), not \(n-1\).

### 05.7 Matrix check: \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}\) give the same estimates Slides L5 p.8 · L2 p.22–25

This section writes a small data set in matrix form and shows that \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}\) contain all the sums that \(S_{xx}\) and \(S_{xy}\) need. Background is in L05 unit 01 and L02 unit 03.

The design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) has one row for each observation. For simple linear regression, row \(i\) is \([\,1,\ x_i\,]\).

The transpose \(\mathbf{X}^T\) is \(\mathbf{X}\) with rows and columns interchanged: \([\mathbf{X}^T]_{ij}=[\mathbf{X}]_{ji}\) (Slides L5 p.9).

In a matrix product \(\mathbf{AB}\), entry \((i,j)\) is row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) times column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{B}\), term by term, then added.

Data (Added): \(n=4\), \(x=(1,2,4,5)\), \(y=(2,3,7,8)\).

1

Write \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) L5 p.8

\[\mathbf{y}=\left[\begin{array}{c}2\\3\\7\\8\end{array}\right],\qquad \mathbf{X}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}1&1\\1&2\\1&4\\1&5\end{array}\right]\]

2

Calculate \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) (2 × 2)

\[\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}n&\sum x_i\\\sum x_i&\sum x_i^2\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}4&1+2+4+5\\1+2+4+5&1+4+16+25\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}4&12\\12&46\end{array}\right]\]

3

Calculate \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}\) (2 × 1)

\[\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}=\left[\begin{array}{c}\sum y_i\\\sum x_iy_i\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}2+3+7+8\\2+6+28+40\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}20\\76\end{array}\right]\]

Means from the entries

\[\bar x=\frac{12}{4}=3,\qquad\bar y=\frac{20}{4}=5\]

\(S_{xx}\) and \(S_{xy}\) from the entries L2 p.25

Terms used in the questions · Added

Standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\): the estimated standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\).

\[\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_0}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-1),\qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta_1}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)(-x_i)\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
Set the first derivative to 0 at \((\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)\). Divide by \(-2\). The term \(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i\) is \(e_i\).
\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
Set the second derivative to 0 at \((\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)\). Divide by \(-2\).
\[0=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n x_ie_i\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(b)** Put \(x=\bar x\) into the fitted line. Use \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\) (Lecture 2 · p.26).
\[\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x=(\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x)+\hat\beta_1\bar x=\bar y\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(c)** Divide each \(x_i\) by 1000. Then \(\bar x^*=\bar x/1000\) and \(x_i^*-\bar x^*=(x_i-\bar x)/1000\).
\[S_{xx}^*=\sum\frac{(x_i-\bar x)^2}{1000^2}=\frac{S_{xx}}{10^6},\qquad S_{xy}^*=\sum\frac{(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{1000}=\frac{S_{xy}}{1000}\] \[\hat\beta_1^*=\frac{S_{xy}^*}{S_{xx}^*}=\frac{S_{xy}/1000}{S_{xx}/10^6}=1000\,\hat\beta_1\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\hat\beta_0^*=\bar y-\hat\beta_1^*\bar x^*=\bar y-(1000\,\hat\beta_1)\frac{\bar x}{1000}=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=\hat\beta_0\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
The fitted values do not change: \(\hat\beta_0^*+\hat\beta_1^*x_i^*=\hat\beta_0+1000\hat\beta_1\cdot x_i/1000=\hat y_i\). Thus the residuals \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\) do not change. **[1]**

Marker note: a 1 kg change is a 1000 g change. Thus the slope for each kilogram is 1000 times the slope for each gram.

### 06.3 Question 2 [Theory] · Distribution of \(\hat\beta_1\) and the CI (10 marks) Added Slides L3 p.12–39 Slides L1 p.25–27

This question tests the derivations of Lecture 3 and the meaning of a CI. Review: L03 unit 02, L03 unit 03, L03 unit 04, L03 unit 05.

Question 2 · [Theory] Consider the usual simple linear regression model. Write \(\hat\beta_1=\sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i\) with \(w_i=\dfrac{x_i-\bar x}{S_{xx}}\) and \(S_{xx}=\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)^2\).
(a) Show that \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i=0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i=1\). Use these results to show that \(E[\hat\beta_1]=\beta_1\). **[3 marks]**
(b) Show that \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\). Explain why \(\hat\beta_1\) has a Normal distribution. **[2 marks]**
(c) State the three facts that give \(\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\). Show the step that removes \(\sigma\). **[2 marks]**
(d) With \(n=15\), a researcher gets the 95% CI \((0.40,\ 2.00)\) for \(\beta_1\).
(i) Find \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). **[1 mark]**
(ii) True or false: "\(P(0.40\le\beta_1\le2.00)=0.95\)". Explain. **[1 mark]**
(iii) Without a new calculation, state the decision of the two-sided test of \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) at \(\alpha=0.05\), and of \(H_0:\beta_1=1.5\) at \(\alpha=0.05\). Explain. **[1 mark]**
Quantile: \(t_{0.975,13}=2.160\).

Show the solution

**(a)** The sum of deviations from the mean is 0.
\[\sum_{i=1}^n w_i=\frac{1}{S_{xx}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=\frac{1}{S_{xx}}(n\bar x-n\bar x)=0\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
Write \(x_i=(x_i-\bar x)+\bar x\). The term with \(\bar x\) is 0 by the result above.
\[\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i=\frac{1}{S_{xx}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)\big[(x_i-\bar x)+\bar x\big]=\frac{S_{xx}+\bar x\cdot0}{S_{xx}}=1\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
The \(w_i\) are constants, because \(x_i\) is fixed. Use linearity of expectation and \(E[y_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) (Lecture 3 · p.14–18).
\[E[\hat\beta_1]=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)=\beta_0\sum w_i+\beta_1\sum w_ix_i=\beta_0\cdot0+\beta_1\cdot1=\beta_1\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(b)** The \(y_i\) are independent with \(\mathrm{Var}[y_i]=\sigma^2\). Thus the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances (Lecture 3 · p.19–23).
\[\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2\sigma^2=\sigma^2\frac{\sum_{i}(x_i-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}^2}=\sigma^2\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
\(\hat\beta_1\) is a linear combination of the independent Normal variables \(y_i\sim N(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2)\). A linear combination of independent Normal variables is Normal (Lecture 1 · p.25; Lecture 3 · p.12). **[1](c)** The three facts (Lecture 3 · p.31): **[1]**

- \(Z=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim N(0,1)\).
- \(V=\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2\sim\chi^2_{(n-2)}\).
- \(Z\) and \(V\) are independent.

For independent \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\), \(Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}\sim t_\nu\) (Lecture 1 · p.27). Use \(\nu=n-2\) and \(\hat\sigma^2=\sum e_i^2/(n-2)\). The \(\sigma\) cancels:
\[\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-2)}}=\frac{(\hat\beta_1-\beta_1)\sqrt{S_{xx}}/\sigma}{\hat\sigma/\sigma}=\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\sim t_{(n-2)}\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(d)(i)** The CI is \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{0.975,13}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). The center is the estimate. The half-width is the quantile times the SE.
\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{0.40+2.00}{2}=1.20,\qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{(2.00-0.40)/2}{2.160}=\frac{0.80}{2.160}=0.3704\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(ii)** False. \(\beta_1\) is a fixed number. It is inside \((0.40, 2.00)\) or it is not. The 95% is a property of the method: in repeated samples, 95% of the intervals contain \(\beta_1\) (Lecture 3 · p.38–39). **[1](iii)** A two-sided test at level 0.05 rejects \(H_0:\beta_1=\theta_0\) exactly when \(\theta_0\) is outside the 95% CI. The value 0 is outside \((0.40,2.00)\): reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\). The value 1.5 is inside: do not reject \(H_0:\beta_1=1.5\). **[1]**

Check for (iii): \(t=(1.20-0)/0.3704=3.240>2.160\), so reject. \(t=(1.20-1.5)/0.3704=-0.810\), and \(|{-0.810}|<2.160\), so do not reject.

### 06.4 Question 3 [Applied] · Battery data: estimates, table, CI, one-sided test (10 marks) Added Slides L2 p.26; L3 p.35–49

This question tests the calculations of Lectures 2–3 and the reading of a coefficient table. Review: L03 unit 04, L03 unit 06.

Question 3 · [Applied] · invented data An engineer tests 8 new batteries of the same model. Battery \(i\) goes through \(100\,x_i\) charge cycles, with \(x_i=1,2,\dots,8\). Then the engineer measures \(y_i\), the remaining capacity as a percentage of the original capacity. The summaries are: \[n=8,\quad \bar x=4.5,\quad \bar y=91.025,\quad S_{xx}=42,\quad S_{xy}=-92.6,\quad \sum_{i=1}^8e_i^2=1.3140.\] The coefficient table for the fit of \(y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i\) is below. Three values are missing.

|  | Estimate | Std. Error | t value | Pr(>|t|)

| (Intercept) | 100.94643 | 0.36464 | 276.84 | 1.50e-13

| cycles | −2.20476 | (i) | (ii) | 8.19e-08

Residual standard error: (iii) on 6 degrees of freedom
(a) Calculate \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_0\) from the summaries. Compare them with the table. **[2 marks]**
(b) Interpret \(\hat\beta_1\) in the context of the study. **[1 mark]**
(c) Calculate the missing values (i), (ii), and (iii). **[3 marks]**
(d) Calculate a 95% CI for \(\beta_1\). **[2 marks]**
(e) The manufacturer states that the capacity drops by at most 2 percentage points for each 100 cycles. Test \(H_0:\beta_1=-2\) against \(H_1:\beta_1<-2\) at \(\alpha=0.05\). Give the test statistic, a range for the p-value, and a conclusion. **[2 marks]**
Quantiles for \(t_6\): \(t_{0.95,6}=1.943\), \(t_{0.975,6}=2.447\), \(t_{0.99,6}=3.143\), \(t_{0.995,6}=3.707\).

Show the solution

**(a)** Use \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\) (Lecture 2 · p.26).
\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{-92.6}{42}=-2.20476\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\hat\beta_0=91.025-(-2.20476)(4.5)=91.025+9.92143=100.94643\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
Both values agree with the Estimate column.

**(b)** For each extra 100 charge cycles, the estimated mean remaining capacity is 2.205 percentage points lower. **[1]**

Marker note: the answer must say "mean" (or "on average"), give the unit of \(x\) (100 cycles), and give the unit of \(y\) (percentage points).

**(c)** (iii) is \(\hat\sigma\), with \(\hat\sigma^2=\sum e_i^2/(n-2)\) (Lecture 2 · p.35).
\[\text{(iii)}\quad \hat\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1.3140}{8-2}}=\sqrt{0.2190}=0.4680\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\text{(i)}\quad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}=\frac{0.4680}{\sqrt{42}}=\frac{0.4680}{6.4807}=0.07221\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\text{(ii)}\quad t=\frac{\hat\beta_1-0}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}=\frac{-2.20476}{0.07221}=-30.53\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
The t value in the table tests \(H_0:\beta_1=0\). Thus \(\theta_0=0\) in (ii).

**(d)** Use \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{0.975,6}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) with \(n-2=6\) (Lecture 3 · p.35).
\[t_{0.975,6}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=2.447\times0.07221=0.1767\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[-2.2048\pm0.1767\ \Longrightarrow\ (-2.3815,\ -2.0281)\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(e)** Use \(\theta_0=-2\). Under \(H_0\), \(T=\dfrac{\hat\beta_1-\theta_0}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}\sim t_{(6)}\) (Lecture 3 · p.41).
\[t=\frac{-2.2048-(-2)}{0.07221}=\frac{-0.2048}{0.07221}=-2.836\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
\(H_1\) says \(\beta_1<-2\). Thus only small values of \(t\) are evidence for \(H_1\), and the p-value uses only the left tail: \(P(T\le-2.836)\) (Lecture 3 · p.48–49). The value 2.836 is between \(t_{0.975,6}=2.447\) and \(t_{0.99,6}=3.143\). Thus \(0.01<p<0.025\). The exact value is 0.0149.

The p-value is less than 0.05. Reject \(H_0\). The data give evidence that the capacity drops by more than 2 percentage points for each 100 cycles. **[1]**

Marker note: a two-sided p-value (between 0.02 and 0.05) gets no mark for the p-value. A critical value method is also correct: reject because \(-2.836<-t_{0.95,6}=-1.943\).

### 06.5 Question 4 [Applied] · Electricity data: mean response and prediction (8 marks) Added Slides L4 p.9–39

This question tests the CI for a mean response and the prediction interval. Review: L04 unit 03, L04 unit 04, L04 unit 05.

Question 4 · [Applied] · invented data An office building records \(y\), its daily electricity use (kWh), on 20 summer weekdays. The covariate \(x\) is the daily maximum outdoor temperature (°C). The temperatures in the data go from 15 °C to 29 °C. The summaries are \(n=20\), \(\bar x=21.45\), \(S_{xx}=346.95\). The fit of \(y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\epsilon_i\) gives:

|  | Estimate | Std. Error | t value | Pr(>|t|)

| (Intercept) | 104.94868 | 22.32235 | 4.702 | 0.000178

| temp | 7.18771 | 1.02159 | 7.036 | 1.45e-06

Residual standard error: 19.03 on 18 degrees of freedom
(a) Estimate the mean electricity use on days with a maximum temperature of 25 °C. **[1 mark]**
(b) Calculate a 95% CI for the mean in (a). **[2 marks]**
(c) Tomorrow the maximum temperature will be 25 °C. Calculate a 95% prediction interval for tomorrow's electricity use. **[2 marks]**
(d) Explain why the interval in (c) is wider than the interval in (b). Suppose that \(n\) becomes very large and \(\sigma\) stays the same. What happens to the width of each interval? **[2 marks]**
(e) A manager wants a prediction for a day with a maximum of 40 °C. Is the model prediction reliable? Explain. **[1 mark]**
Quantiles: \(t_{0.975,18}=2.101\), \(z_{0.975}=1.960\).

Show the solution

**(a)** Use \(\hat\mu_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0\) with \(x_0=25\) (Lecture 4 · p.10).
\[\hat\mu_0=104.94868+7.18771\times25=104.94868+179.69275=284.641\ \text{kWh}\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(b)** Use \(\hat\mu_0\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\) (Lecture 4 · p.19–20).
\[\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}=\frac1{20}+\frac{(25-21.45)^2}{346.95}=0.05+\frac{12.6025}{346.95}=0.05+0.03632=0.08632\] \[\hat\sigma\sqrt{0.08632}=19.03\times0.29381=5.5912\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[284.641\pm2.101\times5.5912=284.641\pm11.747\ \Longrightarrow\ (272.894,\ 296.388)\ \text{kWh}\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(c)** The point prediction is \(\hat y_{new}=284.641\), the same number as \(\hat\mu_0\). Add 1 under the square root (Lecture 4 · p.36–37).
\[\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+0.08632}=19.03\times1.04227=19.8344\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[284.641\pm2.101\times19.8344=284.641\pm41.672\ \Longrightarrow\ (242.969,\ 326.313)\ \text{kWh}\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(d)** The CI covers the uncertainty in \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) only. The PI also covers the new error \(\epsilon_{new}\), which is independent of the data. Thus \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat y_{new}-y_{new}]=\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]+\sigma^2\), and the PI has the extra "1" (Lecture 4 · p.31–35). **[1]**

When \(n\) becomes very large, \(\frac1n\to0\) and \(\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\to0\), because \(S_{xx}\) grows. The CI width goes to 0. The PI width does not go to 0. It goes to approximately \(2\times1.960\,\sigma\). With \(\hat\sigma=19.03\), the half-width is approximately \(1.960\times19.03=37.30\) kWh. **[1]**

Here the CI width is \(2\times11.747=23.494\) kWh and the PI width is \(2\times41.672=83.344\) kWh.

**(e)** No. The value 40 °C is outside the range of the data (15 °C to 29 °C). The data cannot show that the line stays straight at 40 °C. This is extrapolation. The formula gives \(104.94868+7.18771\times40=392.457\) kWh, but the PI from the formula does not include the risk that the model is wrong there. **[1]**

### 06.6 Question 5 [Theory] · \(\bar y\), \(\hat\beta_1\), and \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]\) with matrices (8 marks) Added Slides L4 p.9–18; L5 p.26–40, 45

This question joins Lecture 4 and Lecture 5. It uses matrix rules to prove a step of the mean-response derivation. Review: L04 unit 02, L05 unit 04, L05 unit 05.

Question 5 · [Theory] In matrix form the simple linear regression model is \(\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta,\ \sigma^2\mathbf{I})\), where row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{X}\) is \((1,\ x_i)\). Let \(\mathbf{a}=(1/n,\dots,1/n)^T\) and \(\mathbf{w}=(w_1,\dots,w_n)^T\) with \(w_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}\). Then \(\bar y=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}\) and \(\hat\beta_1=\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{y}\).
(a) Use \(\mathrm{cov}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y},\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{V}\mathbf{b}\) with \(\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\) to show that \(\mathrm{cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1)=0\). **[3 marks]**
(b) Show that \(\bar y\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are independent. **[2 marks]**
(c) Use \(\hat\mu_0=\bar y+\hat\beta_1(x_0-\bar x)\) to derive \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]=\sigma^2\left(\dfrac1n+\dfrac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\). **[2 marks]**
(d) For which \(x_0\) is the 95% CI for \(\mu_0\) the narrowest? Give the variance of \(\hat\mu_0\) at that \(x_0\). **[1 mark]**

Show the solution

**(a)** From the model, \(\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})=\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\) (Lecture 5 · p.45). **[1]**
\[\mathrm{cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1)=\mathbf{a}^T(\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{w}=\sigma^2\,\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{w}=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1n\,w_i\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\,S_{xx}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\,S_{xx}}\cdot0=0\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(b)** Stack the two estimators: \((\bar y,\hat\beta_1)^T=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\), where \(\mathbf{C}\) is the \(2\times n\) matrix with rows \(\mathbf{a}^T\) and \(\mathbf{w}^T\). By linearity of the MVN (Lecture 5 · p.37), \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\sim MVN(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta,\ \sigma^2\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^T)\). **[1]**
\[\sigma^2\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^T=\sigma^2\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}&\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{w}\\ \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{a}&\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{w}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\sigma^2/n&0\\ 0&\sigma^2/S_{xx}\end{array}\right]\]
The off-diagonal entry is 0 by (a). For an MVN vector, a zero covariance gives independence (Lecture 5 · p.40). Thus \(\bar y\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are independent. **[1]**

Marker note: "covariance 0" alone does not give independence. The answer must say that the pair is MVN. Lecture 5 · p.41–44 shows two Normal variables that are not MVN.

**(c)** \(x_0-\bar x\) is a constant. Use \(\mathrm{Var}(U+cW)=\mathrm{Var}(U)+c^2\mathrm{Var}(W)+2c\,\mathrm{cov}(U,W)\), with \(\mathrm{Var}[\bar y]=\sigma^2/n\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\beta_1]=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\). **[1]**
\[\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+(x_0-\bar x)^2\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}+2(x_0-\bar x)\cdot0=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(d)** The term \((x_0-\bar x)^2\ge0\) is smallest, and equal to 0, at \(x_0=\bar x\). There \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat\mu_0]=\sigma^2/n\), and the CI is the narrowest. **[1]**

### 06.7 Question 6 [Applied] · Mean vector, covariance matrix, and MVN (7 marks) Added Slides L5 p.22–40

This question tests the matrix calculations of Lecture 5. Review: L05 unit 04, L05 unit 05, L05 unit 06.

Question 6 · [Applied] Let \(\mathbf{y}=(y_1,y_2,y_3)^T\sim MVN(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) with \[\boldsymbol\mu=\left[\begin{array}{c}2\\-1\\3\end{array}\right],\qquad \boldsymbol\Sigma=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}4&1&0\\1&2&-1\\0&-1&3\end{array}\right].\] (a) Let \(u=y_1+2y_2+y_3\). Calculate \(E[u]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(u)\). **[2 marks]**
(b) Let \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\) with \(\mathbf{A}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1&1&0\\1&-2&-1\end{array}\right]\). Calculate \(E[\mathbf{z}]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z})\). **[2 marks]**
(c) Give the distribution of \(z_1\). Find the value \(c\) with \(P(z_1\le c)=0.95\). **[2 marks]**
(d) Are \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) independent? Explain. **[1 mark]**
Quantile: \(z_{0.95}=1.645\).

Show the solution

**(a)** Write \(u=\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}\) with \(\mathbf{a}=(1,2,1)^T\). Use \(E[\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{a}^T\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{a}^T\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{a}\) (Lecture 5 · p.27–29).
\[E[u]=1(2)+2(-1)+1(3)=3\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{a}=\left[\begin{array}{c}4+2+0\\1+4-1\\0-2+3\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}6\\4\\1\end{array}\right],\qquad \mathrm{Var}(u)=\mathbf{a}^T(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{a})=1(6)+2(4)+1(1)=15\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(b)** Use \(E[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{y}]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{A}^T\) (Lecture 5 · p.30–35).
\[E[\mathbf{z}]=\left[\begin{array}{c}2-1+0\\2+2-3\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}1\\1\end{array}\right]\quad\textbf{[1]}\] \[\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}5&3&-1\\2&-2&-1\end{array}\right],\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{A}^T=\left[\begin{array}{cc}5+3+0&5-6+1\\2-2+0&2+4+1\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}8&0\\0&7\end{array}\right]\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(c)** \(\mathbf{z}\) is MVN by linearity (Lecture 5 · p.37), and a single entry of an MVN vector is Normal (marginal, Lecture 5 · p.38). Thus \(z_1\sim N(1,\ 8)\). **[1]**
\[c=1+z_{0.95}\sqrt8=1+1.645\times2.8284=1+4.653=5.653\quad\textbf{[1]}\]
**(d)** Yes. \(\mathbf{z}\) is MVN and \(\mathrm{cov}(z_1,z_2)=0\) (the off-diagonal entry of \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z})\)). For an MVN vector, zero covariance gives independence (Lecture 5 · p.40). **[1]**

Check: \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is symmetric, and \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z})\) is symmetric with positive diagonal entries, as a covariance matrix must be (Lecture 5 · p.24–25).
